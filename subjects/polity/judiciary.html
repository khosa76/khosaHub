1. Introduction & Separation of Powers


  • Independent Organ of Government: Under the Indian Constitutional scheme, the Judiciary is an independent and integrated organ, protected from interference or control by the Executive or Legislative branches.
  • Single Integrated Judicial System: Unlike the dual judiciary of the USA (Federal Courts & State Courts), India has a single integrated hierarchy of courts enforcing both Central and State laws.

Key Rules for Advocates on Record (AOR):

  • Applies ONLY to the Supreme Court of India.
  • No case can be admitted to the Supreme Court without AOR approval.
  • Only an Advocate on Record (AOR) can file a case or petition in the Supreme Court.

Key Historical & Constitutional Facts

  • Advocate directly elevated as Supreme Court Judge: S.M. Sikri (Advocate General of Punjab → SC Judge → Chief Justice of India).
  • Basic Structure Doctrine: Established in the landmark Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973) ruling. Judicial Independence and Judicial Review are core components of the Basic Structure.

3. Appointment of Judges & The Judges Cases Timeline


Evolution of Judicial Appointment (The 4 Judges Cases)

  1. First Judges Case: S.P. Gupta v. Union of India (1981)
    • Executive Primacy: Ruling held that "consultation" with the CJI does not mean "concurrence" and is not binding on the President.
  2. Second Judges Case: SC Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India (1993)
    • Judicial Primacy: Reversed the 1981 decision. Ruled that "consultation" means CONCURRENCE and is binding on the Executive.
  3. Third Judges Case (1998 - Advisory Opinion under Art 143):
    • Established the Collegium System. Formalized recommendation structures for judicial appointments.
  4. Fourth Judges Case (2015):
    • The Supreme Court struck down the 99th Constitutional Amendment Act (2014) and the NJAC (National Judicial Appointments Commission) Act as unconstitutional, restoring the Collegium System.

4. Removal / Impeachment Process (Judges Inquiry Act, 1968)


  • Grounds for Removal: A Judge of the Supreme Court or High Court can be removed only on two grounds: (1) Proved Misbehavior or (2) Incapacity.
  • Judges Inquiry Act, 1968: Regulates the detailed procedure relating to the removal of judges.

7-Step Impeachment Workflow

  1. Initiation: Removal motion signed by at least 100 MPs in Lok Sabha OR 50 MPs in Rajya Sabha submitted to Presiding Officer.
  2. Admission: Speaker/Chairman may admit or reject the motion.
  3. 3-Member Inquiry Committee: If admitted, a 3-member committee is constituted:
    • CJI or a Senior SC Judge
    • Chief Justice of a High Court
    • A Distinguished Jurist
  4. Investigation: The Committee investigates charges and presents findings to Parliament.
  5. If Committee Finds NOT GUILTY: The finding is BINDING & FINAL. Motion drops immediately; no further impeachment possible.
  6. If Committee Finds GUILTY: Finding is not automatically binding on Parliament. Both Houses take up motion for voting.
  7. Special Majority & Presidential Order: Motion must be passed in each House by a Special Majority ($\frac{1}{2}\text{ of Total Membership} + \frac{2}{3}\text{ Present & Voting}$). Upon address by Parliament, the President issues removal order.

5. Powers & Jurisdictions of Courts


1. Original Jurisdiction (Article 131)

  • Supreme Court Exclusive Original Jurisdiction (Article 131): Decides federal disputes in the first instance:
    • Center vs. One or more States
    • Center + State(s) vs. State(s)
    • State vs. State
  • High Court Original Jurisdiction: Enforcement of Fundamental Rights, Revenue matters, and Disputes regarding Election of MPs & MLAs (e.g., Indira Gandhi v. Raj Narain, 1975).

2. Appellate Jurisdiction & Special Leave Petition (SLP - Article 136)

  • Appellate Jurisdiction: Hears appeals in Constitutional, Civil, and Criminal matters.
  • Special Leave Petition (SLP - Article 136): Plenary discretionary power of the Supreme Court to grant special leave to appeal against any judgment, decree, or order in any cause or matter passed by any court or tribunal in India (except Military Tribunals). Landmark Case: Arnesh Kumar v. State of Bihar.

3. Advisory Jurisdiction (Article 143)

  • General Law or Public Importance Matter: Supreme Court may refuse to provide advisory opinion (e.g., Same-sex marriage reference).
  • Pre-Constitutional Treaties / Agreements (Prior to 1950): Supreme Court CANNOT refuse to tender advice.

4. Writ Jurisdiction: Supreme Court (Art 32) vs. High Court (Art 226)

Parameter Supreme Court (Article 32) High Court (Article 226)
Nature of Right Article 32 is itself a Fundamental Right Article 226 is a Constitutional / Discretionary Right
Jurisdictional Scope Narrower Scope: Issued ONLY for Fundamental Rights violations Wider Scope: Issued for Fundamental Rights AND ordinary legal rights

6. Judicial Review, Activism & Restraint


Scope of Judicial Review: India vs. USA

🌐 Scope of Judicial Review: India vs. USA

'Due Process of Law'"] Ind --> MG["⚖️ Landmark Case: Maneka Gandhi (1978)
India adopted 'Due Process' principles in practice"] US --> MG

  • Procedure Established by Law: Examines law strictly on procedural grounds (whether Parliament followed constitutional procedure). Reasonableness of law substance is not judged.
  • Due Process of Law: Examines law on both procedural AND substantive grounds (if arbitrary, unfair, or unreasonable, court strikes it down even if passed with procedure).

Judicial Activism vs. Restraint & PIL

  • Pioneers of Judicial Activism in India: Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer and Justice P.N. Bhagwati.
  • Relaxation of Locus Standi: Under traditional rule, only the aggrieved person could approach court. Under relaxed Locus Standi, any public-spirited citizen or social organization can approach court on behalf of oppressed sections.
  • Public Interest Litigation (PIL): Also referred to as SAL (Social Action Litigation), SIL (Social Interest Litigation), or CAL (Class Action Litigation).


7. Tribunals (Articles 323A & 323B)


  • Constitutional Origin: Not part of the original 1950 Constitution. Added via the 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976 based on recommendations of the Swaran Singh Committee.
  • Article 323A: Administrative Tribunals ONLY (CAT – Central Administrative Tribunal & SAT – State Administrative Tribunals). Can be created only by Parliament.
  • Article 323B: Tribunals for other matters (Taxation, Foreign Exchange, Industrial/Labour, Land Reforms, NCLAT, Armed Forces Tribunal, NGT). Created by Parliament or State Legislatures.

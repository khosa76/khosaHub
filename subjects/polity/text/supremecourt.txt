Supreme Court of India
Judiciary in India vs USA
● India has a unified and integrated judicial
system with the Supreme Court at the
top, followed by High Courts and
subordinate courts like district and lower
courts.
● This system was adopted from the
Government of India Act, 1935.
● Both central and state laws are enforced
by the same hierarchy of courts.
● In contrast, the USA follows a dual
system:
○ Federal laws are enforced by
federal courts.
○ State laws are enforced by state
judiciary.
● Hence, although India is federal, it has a
unified judiciary and one system of justice
unlike the USA.
Supreme Court of India – Historical Background
● Inaugurated on: January 28, 1950.
● It succeeded the Federal Court of India
(created under Government of India Act,
1935).
● Also replaced the British Privy Council as
the highest appellate authority.
● Jurisdiction: Broader than the Federal
Court.
● Articles 124 to 147 (Part V) of the
Constitution deal with:
○ Organization
○ Independence
○ Jurisdiction
○ Powers
○ Procedures
● Parliament is empowered to regulate these
aspects.
Composition of Supreme Court
● Original strength (1950): 1 Chief Justice +
7 Judges = 8 total.
● Current strength: 1 Chief Justice + 33
Judges = 34 total.
● Expansion Timeline:
○ 1956 – 10 Judges
○ 1960 – 13 Judges
○ 1977 – 17 Judges
○ 1986 – 25 Judges
○ 2008 – 30 Judges
○ 2019 – 34 Judges
Appointment of Judges
● By the President of India.
● Chief Justice: Appointed after consultation
with judges of SC and High Courts as
deemed necessary.
● Other Judges: Appointed after consultation
with Chief Justice + other judges of SC/HC
as deemed necessary.
● Consultation with CJI is mandatory
(except when appointing the CJI himself).
Judges Cases: Evolution of
"Consultation"
First Judges Case (1981)
● Consultation ≠ Concurrence.
● Meant only exchange of views.
Second Judges Case (1993)
● Overruled 1981 ruling.
● Consultation = Concurrence.
● CJI’s advice is binding on the President.
● CJI to consult 2 seniormost judges before
giving advice.
Third Judges Case (1998)
● Consultation must be with a collegium of 4
seniormost judges.
● If 2 judges dissent, CJI must not forward
the recommendation.
● Sole opinion of CJI is not valid.
NJAC & the Fourth Judges Case
99th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2014 +
NJAC Act, 2014
● Replaced Collegium system with National
Judicial Appointments Commission
(NJAC).
● NJAC included non-judicial members.
Fourth Judges Case (2015)
● Supreme Court struck down both:
○ 99th Amendment
○ NJAC Act
● Held NJAC violates judicial
independence.
● Collegium system restored.
Appointment of Chief Justice of India
(CJI)
● Convention till 1973: Seniormost SC judge
appointed as CJI.
● Broken in 1973: A.N. Ray appointed as CJI
superseding 3 senior judges.
● Broken again in 1977: M.U. Beg
appointed, superseding seniormost judge.
● Second Judges Case (1993): SC ruled that
seniormost judge must be appointed as
CJI.
Qualifications for Appointment (Article
124)
A person is qualified to be appointed as a judge of
the Supreme Court if:
1. Citizen of India
2. Any one of the following:
○ (a) Has been a judge of a High
Court (or multiple HCs in
succession) for at least 5 years, or
○ (b) Has been an advocate of a High
Court (or multiple HCs in
succession) for at least 10 years, or
○ (c) Is a distinguished jurist, in the
opinion of the President of India
✅ Note:
● The Constitution does not prescribe a
minimum age for appointment.
● The “distinguished jurist” clause has rarely
been invoked.
Oath or Affirmation (Article 124,
Schedule III)
Before entering office, every judge of the Supreme
Court must take an oath or affirmation before the
President or someone appointed by the President.
The judge swears to:
1. Bear true faith and allegiance to the
Constitution of India
2. Uphold the sovereignty and integrity of
India
3. Perform duties honestly, to the best of
ability, knowledge, and judgment — without
fear or favour, affection or ill-will
4. Uphold the Constitution and the laws
Salaries, Allowances & Pension
● Determined by the Parliament.
● Cannot be reduced to their disadvantage
after appointment, except during a
financial emergency (Article 360).
As per 2018 revision:
● Chief Justice of India (CJI): ₹2.80
lakh/month (earlier ₹1 lakh)
● Other Judges: ₹2.50 lakh/month (earlier
₹90,000)
● Also entitled to:
○ Sumptuary allowance
○ Free official residence
○ Medical facilities
○ Official car and telephone, etc.
Post-Retirement Pension:
● Retired Chief Justice and Judges are
entitled to 50% of their last drawn salary
as monthly pension.
Tenure and Removal of Supreme Court Judges
Tenure of Judges (Article 124)
Although the Constitution does not specify a fixed
tenure, it lays down three provisions:
1. Retirement Age:
○ Judge holds office until the age of
65 years.
○ Any dispute about age is decided by
an authority specified by
Parliament.
2. Resignation:
○ Judge may resign by writing to the
President of India.
3. Removal:
○ Can be removed by the President,
but only on Parliament's
recommendation under a specified
procedure.
Removal (Impeachment) Procedure
● A judge of the Supreme Court can be
removed only on grounds of:
1. Proven misbehaviour, or
2. Incapacity
● Process regulated by: The Judges
(Inquiry) Act, 1968
Steps of Removal:
1. Initiation of Motion
○ Must be signed by:
■ 100 MPs in Lok Sabha, or
■ 50 MPs in Rajya Sabha
○ Submitted to Speaker (LS) or
Chairman (RS)
2. Admittance of Motion
○ Speaker/Chairman may admit or
reject the motion.
3. Formation of Inquiry Committee
○ If admitted, a 3-member committee
is formed:
■ (a) Chief Justice or SC judge
■ (b) Chief Justice of a High
Court
■ (c) A distinguished jurist
4. Inquiry
○ Committee investigates and gives
report:
■ If guilty, the motion
proceeds.
■ If not guilty, the matter is
dropped.
5. Parliamentary Voting
○ Each House must pass the motion
with a special majority:
■ Majority of total membership,
and
■ At least two-thirds of
members present and
voting
6. President’s Order
○ Once both Houses pass the motion,
an address is presented to the
President, who then issues the
removal order.
Important Points
● The term “impeachment” is not used for
judges in the Constitution (used only for
President’s removal), but commonly used
in this context.
● No Supreme Court judge has ever been
successfully removed.
Case Study: Justice V. Ramaswami
(1991–1993)
● First-ever case of attempted removal.
● The Enquiry Committee found him guilty
of misbehaviour.
● However, the motion failed in Lok Sabha
due to abstention by the Congress Party,
despite majority support.
● Hence, he continued in office.
Acting, Ad Hoc, and Retired Judges of the
Supreme Court
1. Acting Chief Justice
The President can appoint a judge of the
Supreme Court as Acting Chief Justice of India
under the following conditions:
● When the office of the Chief Justice of
India is vacant.
● When the CJI is temporarily absent.
● When the CJI is unable to perform duties
due to any reason.
✅ Such appointment ensures continuity in
leadership at the top of the judiciary.
2. Ad Hoc Judge
When there is a shortage of quorum of permanent
judges to hold or continue a session of the
Supreme Court:
● The Chief Justice of India (CJI) may
appoint a High Court judge as an ad hoc
judge for a temporary period.
Conditions:
● Consultation with the Chief Justice of the
concerned High Court is mandatory.
● Prior consent of the President is required.
● The appointee must be qualified to be a
Supreme Court judge.
Powers & Duties:
● Must attend SC sittings in priority to
his/her High Court duties.
● While attending SC:
○ Enjoys all powers, jurisdiction,
and privileges of an SC judge.
○ Performs full duties as an SC
judge.
3. Retired Judges
At any time, the Chief Justice of India may
request a retired judge to act as a judge of the
Supreme Court.
Eligibility:
● Can be a retired judge of the SC or a
High Court.
● Must be qualified for appointment as an SC
judge.
Conditions:
● Requires:
○ Consent of the President, and
○ Consent of the retired judge.
Rights & Restrictions:
● Will receive allowances as determined by
the President.
● Will enjoy all powers and privileges of an
SC judge while serving.
● However, will not be treated as a
permanent judge of the SC for any other
purposes.
Seat and Procedure of the Supreme Court
Seat of the Supreme Court (Article 130)
● The Constitution declares Delhi as the
permanent seat of the Supreme Court.
● However, it allows for other places to be
designated as SC benches, but:
Conditions:
○ Chief Justice of India (CJI) can
appoint another seat, but only with
the prior approval of the
President.
○ This provision is optional, not
mandatory.
✅ No court can compel the CJI or the President
to shift or establish an alternate SC bench.
Procedure of the Supreme Court
● The Supreme Court has the power, with
the approval of the President, to frame its
own rules regulating:
○ Practice
○ Procedure of the court
Benches:
● Constitutional Bench (Minimum 5 judges)
is required to:
○ Decide Constitutional matters.
○ Decide Presidential references
under Article 143.
● Other cases:
○ Can be heard by single judges or
division benches (2 or 3 judges)
depending on the nature and
complexity.
Judgements:
● All judgments are pronounced in open
court.
● Decisions are made by majority vote.
● Dissenting opinions can be recorded by
individual judges if they disagree with the
majority.
Independence of the Supreme Court of India
The Supreme Court plays a vital role in India's
democratic and constitutional system as:
● The federal court
● The highest court of appeal
● The guarantor of Fundamental Rights
● The guardian of the Constitution
➡️ To discharge these duties effectively, the
Supreme Court must function independently and
impartially, without executive or legislative
pressure.
Constitutional Safeguards for Judicial
Independence
1. Mode of Appointment
● Judges are appointed by the President in
consultation with the judiciary (SC and HC
judges).
● This limits executive discretion and
avoids political appointments.
2. Security of Tenure
● Judges do not hold office at the pleasure
of the President.
● Can only be removed via a rigorous
impeachment process (as per the
Constitution).
● This ensures stability and impartiality.
3. Fixed Service Conditions
● Salaries, allowances, and pensions are
fixed by Parliament.
● Cannot be altered to the judge’s
disadvantage during their term (except
during a financial emergency).
● Ensures independence through financial
security.
4. Expenses Charged on Consolidated Fund of
India
● All expenses of the Supreme Court (judges’
salaries, pensions, and staff costs) are
charged on the Consolidated Fund.
● These are non-votable by Parliament
(though can be discussed).
● Prevents Parliament from using financial
control as a pressure tool.
5. Conduct of Judges Cannot Be Discussed in
Legislature
● Parliament or State Legislatures cannot
discuss conduct of SC judges except in
the case of impeachment proceedings.
● Prevents political interference in judicial
work.
6. Ban on Practice After Retirement
● Retired SC judges cannot plead or appear
in any court or authority in India.
● Ensures judicial integrity and prevents
**bias
Jurisdiction and Powers of the Supreme Court
of India
The Supreme Court of India holds a unique
position in the Indian constitutional system. It is:
● A Federal Court (like the US Supreme
Court)
● A Final Court of Appeal (like the UK’s
House of Lords)
● Guarantor of Fundamental Rights
● Final Interpreter of the Constitution
● Also exercises advisory and supervisory
functions
➡️ As rightly stated by Alladi Krishnaswamy
Ayyar:
“The Supreme Court of India has more powers
than any other Supreme Court in the world.”
Types of Jurisdiction and Powers of
Supreme Court
1. Original Jurisdiction
2. Writ Jurisdiction
3. Appellate Jurisdiction
4. Advisory Jurisdiction
5. Court of Record
6. Judicial Review
7. Constitutional Interpretation
8. Other Powers
1. Original Jurisdiction (Article 131)
This refers to the power to hear disputes in the
first instance (i.e., not on appeal).
✅ Scope:
Supreme Court has exclusive original
jurisdiction over disputes:
● (a) Between Centre and one or more
States
● (b) Between Centre and one or more
States on one side, and one or more
States on the other side
● (c) Between two or more States
➡️ Exclusive = Only SC can hear it
➡️ Original = Heard at SC for the first time (not
appeal)
🛑 Limitations / Exclusions from Original
Jurisdiction:
1. Disputes must involve a legal right — not
political questions
2. Private citizens cannot bring cases under
this jurisdiction
It does not apply to:
● (a) Treaties, agreements, or
engagements made before Constitution's
commencement, if they exclude SC's
jurisdiction
● (b) Inter-state river water disputes
● (c) Disputes referred to the Finance
Commission
● (d) Disputes regarding adjustments of
certain expenses or pensions between
Centre and States
Case Example – First Original
Jurisdiction Case (1961)
● Case: State of West Bengal vs. Union of
India
● Issue: Constitutionality of the Coal Bearing
Areas (Acquisition and Development)
Act, 1957
● Verdict: SC upheld the Act, dismissed the
state's suit
Writ Jurisdiction of the Supreme Court
The Constitution makes the Supreme Court the
protector and guarantor of Fundamental Rights.
Under Article 32, the SC is empowered to issue
writs for the enforcement of Fundamental
Rights.
Types of Writs Issued by the Supreme
Court
1. Habeas Corpus – “You may have the body”
○ Issued to produce a person
wrongfully detained
2. Mandamus – “We command”
○ Issued to compel a public authority
to perform its duty
3. Prohibition –
○ Issued to stop a lower court from
exceeding its jurisdiction
4. Certiorari –
○ Issued to quash an order passed
by a lower court/tribunal
5. Quo Warranto – “By what authority”
○ Issued to challenge the legal right
of a person to hold a public office
Nature of Jurisdiction under Article 32
● It is a type of original jurisdiction,
meaning:
○ An aggrieved citizen can directly
approach the Supreme Court (no
need to go through lower courts).
● However, this writ jurisdiction is not
exclusive to the SC.
Concurrent Jurisdiction with High
Courts (Article 226)
● High Courts can also issue writs for:
○ Enforcement of Fundamental
Rights, and
○ For any other legal purpose (e.g.,
legal rights, administrative justice)
✅ Therefore:
● Writ jurisdiction of High Courts is wider
than that of the Supreme Court.
● SC: Writs only for Fundamental Rights
● HC: Writs for Fundamental Rights and legal
rights

Appellate and Advisory Jurisdiction of the
Supreme Court of India
Appellate Jurisdiction
The Supreme Court of India is the highest
appellate court, having succeeded the Federal
Court of India and replaced the British Privy
Council.
It hears appeals against the judgments of High
Courts, classified under four categories:
(a) Appeals in Constitutional Matters (Article
132)
● Appeal lies from a High Court judgment if:
1. The High Court certifies the case
involves a substantial question of
law requiring interpretation of the
Constitution.
2. The party aggrieved can then appeal
to the SC on the ground that the
interpretation was incorrect.
(b) Appeals in Civil Matters (Article 133)
● Civil appeal lies to the SC from a High Court
if the HC certifies:
1. The case involves a substantial
question of law of general
importance.
2. The question needs a decision by
the Supreme Court.
✅ Earlier, a monetary limit of ₹20,000 was
required for appeal, but this was removed by the
30th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1972.
(c) Appeals in Criminal Matters (Article 134)
Appeal to SC is allowed from High Court’s
judgment if:
1. As a matter of right, when:
○ (i) The HC reverses an acquittal
and sentences the person to
death.
○ (ii) The HC withdraws a case from
a subordinate court and sentences
the accused to death.
2. With certificate of HC, when:
○ (iii) The HC certifies that the case is
fit for appeal to the SC.
1970 Amendment: Appeal extended to cases
where:
● Conviction is for life imprisonment or 10
years, even if no death penalty involved.
(d) Appeal by Special Leave (Article 136)
● Supreme Court can grant Special Leave to
Appeal from any court or tribunal (except
military courts).
Key Features:
1. Discretionary power – not a matter of
right.
2. Can be against any judgment, final or
interim.
3. Applies to any subject – constitutional,
criminal, civil, taxation, labour, etc.
4. Not limited to High Courts – can apply to
any court or tribunal.
Supreme Court has said:
"This is an exceptional and overriding
power to be exercised sparingly and
only in extraordinary situations."
Advisory Jurisdiction (Article 143)
The President of India may seek the opinion of
the Supreme Court in two situations:
(a) Legal or factual question of public
importance
● May arise or is likely to arise.
● SC may choose to give or refuse the
opinion.
(b) Dispute from pre-Constitution treaties,
agreements, etc.
● These are excluded from SC’s original
jurisdiction.
● In this case, SC is bound to give its opinion.
✅ Key Points:
● The opinion given by SC under Article 143
is advisory only.
● It is not binding on the President.
● However, it provides the Government with a
legally authoritative opinion.
Remaining Jurisdictions and Powers of the
Supreme Court of India
A. Court of Record (Article 129)
As a Court of Record, the Supreme Court has two
key powers:
1. Records of Judgements
○ All judgements, proceedings, and
acts are recorded for perpetual
testimony.
○ These records are of evidentiary
value and cannot be questioned in
any court.
○ They serve as legal precedents
and authoritative references.
2. Power to Punish for Contempt
○ SC can punish for contempt of
itself and also of High Courts,
subordinate courts, and tribunals
(as ruled in 1991).
Contempt of Courts Act, 1971
● Based on the H.N. Sanyal Committee
Report (1963).
Types of Contempt:
● Civil Contempt:
○ Wilful disobedience of court orders
or undertakings.
● Criminal Contempt:
○ Acts that:
■ (i) Scandalise or lower the
authority of the court
■ (ii) Prejudice or interfere with
judicial proceedings
■ (iii) Obstruct the
administration of justice
❌ Not Considered Contempt:
● Innocent publication or distribution
● Fair and accurate report of judicial
proceedings
● Fair criticism of judicial acts
Punishment:
● Simple imprisonment up to 6 months or
● Fine up to ₹2,000, or both
✅ No proceedings can be initiated after 1 year
from the alleged act.
❌ The Act does not apply to Nyaya Panchayats
or village courts.
B. Judicial Review (Basic Structure
Doctrine)
● Power to examine the constitutionality of
legislative laws and executive orders of
both Centre and States.
● If found ultra vires (against the
Constitution), they are declared:
○ Illegal
○ Unconstitutional
○ Void
✅ Such laws or orders cannot be enforced.
C. Review Jurisdiction (Article 137)
● SC can review its own judgements or
orders.
● Time limit: Review petition must be filed
within 30 days of the judgement/order.
Grounds for Review:
1. Discovery of new and important evidence
2. Error apparent on the face of the record
3. Any other sufficient reason
Review must be heard by same judge or bench
that delivered the original judgement.
Curative Petition
Even after a review is dismissed, SC may
reconsider the matter via a curative petition on
these limited grounds:
1. Violation of natural justice
2. Gross miscarriage of justice
3. Abuse of court process
4. Bias of the judge
D. Other Important Powers
1. Disputes related to Presidential and
Vice-Presidential Elections
○ SC has original, exclusive, and
final jurisdiction.
2. Inquiry into UPSC/SPSC Members
○ On a reference from the President,
SC investigates misconduct.
○ If found guilty, SC's advice is
binding for their removal.
3. Transfer or Withdrawal of Cases
○ SC can:
■ Withdraw cases from HCs
and dispose of them directly.
■ Transfer cases from one HC
to another.
4. Binding Nature of Law (Article 141)
○ SC’s law is binding on all courts in
India.
○ Its orders/decrees are enforceable
nationwide.
5. Judicial Superintendence
○ SC exercises supervision and
control over all courts and tribunals
across the country.
6. Extension of Jurisdiction (Articles
138–139)
○ Parliament can expand SC's
jurisdiction for matters under:
■ Union List, or
■ Other matters via special
agreement between Centre
and States.

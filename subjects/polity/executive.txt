Election of the President
✅ Indirect Election by Electoral College
President is not directly elected by the people but
by an Electoral College, comprising:
1. Elected members of both Houses of
Parliament (Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha)
2. Elected members of the legislative
assemblies of states
3. Elected members of the legislative
assemblies of Union Territories of Delhi
and Puducherry
→ Added by: 70th Constitutional
Amendment Act, 1992
→ Effective from: June 1, 1995
❌ Members NOT eligible to vote in
Presidential Elections:
● Nominated members of both Houses of
Parliament
● Nominated members of State Legislative
Assemblies
● Elected and nominated members of
Legislative Councils (in bicameral
legislatures)
● Nominated members of Delhi and
Puducherry Assemblies
104th Constitutional Amendment Act,
2019
● Discontinued the nomination of
Anglo-Indian members to:
○ Lok Sabha
○ State Legislative Assemblies
● Came into effect on 25th January 2020
● Special representation ceased
permanently
⚠️ Effect of Assembly Dissolution
● If a legislative assembly is dissolved, its
members lose their voting rights in the
presidential election.
● Even if fresh elections are not yet held,
these members cannot vote.
Vote Value Calculation for
Presidential Election
Objective:
Ensure:
● Uniformity in representation of different
states
● Parity between the states and the Union
1. Value of Vote of an MLA
Value of MLA’s Vote=(Total population of stateTotal
elected members of State Assembly)÷1000
● The population is based on the 1971
Census until changed by Parliament.
● Only multiples of 1000 are considered.
2. Value of Vote of an MP
Value of MP’s Vote=Total value of votes of all MLAs
of all statesTotal number of elected MPs in both
Houses
● Ensures that collective vote value of MPs
≈ collective vote value of MLAs
● Achieves parity between States and
Union
Illustrative Components in Electoral
College
● Each MLA: Vote value based on state's
population and assembly strength
● Each MP: Vote value derived from total
MLA votes / total elected MPs
● Total Value:
○ All MLAs' votes (State-wise totals)
○ All MPs' votes
○ Grand Total = Votes of all members
of Electoral College
Election Procedure of the
President – Detailed
Mechanics
System Followed
● The election is conducted using the system
of Proportional Representation by means
of Single Transferable Vote (STV).
● Voting is by secret ballot.
Purpose of the System
● Ensures that the successful candidate is
elected by an absolute majority of valid
votes.
● Promotes consensus over confrontation
in the election.
Electoral Quota Calculation
To be declared elected, a candidate must secure a
minimum number of votes, called the Electoral
Quota.
Electoral Quota=Total valid votes polled1+No. of
candidates+1
● Since only one candidate is elected to the
office of President, the formula simplifies as:
Quota=(Total valid votes1+1)+1
Voting Process
● Each member of the Electoral College is
given one ballot paper.
● Voters are required to mark their
preferences using numbers:
→ 1 for first preference,
→ 2 for second,
→ and so on…
● A voter can indicate preferences for as
many candidates as they wish.
Vote Counting Process
1. First Phase:
○ Count first preference votes of all
candidates.
○ If any candidate secures the
Electoral Quota, he/she is declared
elected.
2. Transfer of Votes (If no one meets the
quota):
○ The candidate with the least
number of first preference votes is
eliminated.
○ His/her ballots are examined for
second preference.
○ These votes are then transferred to
the remaining candidates based on
those preferences.
○ This process continues in rounds
until a candidate reaches the
required quota.
Judicial Oversight: Role of Supreme
Court
● All disputes related to Presidential election
are inquired into and decided by the
Supreme Court.
● The decision of the Supreme Court is
final.
● An election cannot be challenged on the
ground that the electoral college was
incomplete
(i.e., vacancy among members is not a
valid ground for invalidation).
If Election is Declared Void
● If the Supreme Court declares the election
void, then:
○ All acts done by the President
before such declaration remain
valid and are not invalidated.
Additional Constitutional
Reference
84th Constitutional Amendment Act,
2001
● Until the figures of the first census after
2026 are published,
● The expression ‘population’ in the vote
value calculation refers to the 1971
Census.
Why Indirect Election for the
President?
✅ Reasons Chosen by Constitution
Makers
1. Harmony with Parliamentary System:
○ The President is only a nominal
executive.
○ Real powers lie with the Council
of Ministers headed by the Prime
Minister.
○ A directly elected President with
no real power would have created a
constitutional anomaly.
2. Cost & Practicality:
○ Direct election would be
expensive, time-consuming, and
energy-draining.
○ Given India’s vast electorate, it
would be impractical for a
symbolic head.
Alternative Proposals in
Constituent Assembly
(Rejected)
● Some members suggested that the
President be elected only by members of
Parliament.
○ Reason for rejection:
■ Parliament may be
dominated by one party,
leading to partisan
Presidents.
■ Would not represent the
States of the Union.
● The current system balances
representation of both Union and States.
Terminology Debate in the
Constituent Assembly
● Objection to the term "Proportional
Representation":
○ Technically applies where multiple
seats are to be filled.
○ Presidential election fills only one
seat, so more accurate term would
be:
■ "Preferential vote system"
or
■ "Alternative vote system".
● Objection to "Single Transferable Vote":
○ Each voter marks multiple
preferences, not casting a single
vote.
○ Therefore, it's not literally a single
vote, but a series of preferences.
Qualifications for Election as
President
A person must satisfy the following to be eligible:
1. Citizen of India
2. Minimum age: 35 years
3. Qualified to be elected as a member of
the Lok Sabha
4. Should not hold any office of profit
under:
○ Union Government
○ State Government
○ Local or Public Authority
Note: The following are not
considered as holding an office of
profit:
● Sitting President or Vice-President
● Governor of any state
● Ministers of the Union or of any state
Nomination Requirements
● Nomination must be:
○ Proposed by at least 50 electors
○ Seconded by at least 50 electors
Security Deposit
● ₹15,000 (in Reserve Bank of India)
● Forfeited if the candidate fails to secure
one-sixth of the total valid votes polled
Before 1997:
● Proposers & seconders: 10 each
● Deposit: ₹2,500
🔁 Changed in 1997 to discourage non-serious
candidates

Conditions of the President’s
Office
(As laid down by the Constitution of India)
1. No Membership of Legislature
● The President should not be a member of:
○ Either House of Parliament
○ A House of any State Legislature
● If elected while being a member, he/she is
deemed to have vacated the seat on the

2. No Other Office of Profit
● The President should not hold any other
office of profit under:
○ Union Government
○ State Government
○ Local Authorities or Public Bodies
3. Official Residence
● Entitled to use of the official residence
without payment of rent:
○ Rashtrapati Bhavan
4. Salary, Allowances & Privileges
● Entitled to emoluments, allowances, and
privileges as determined by Parliament.
5. Protection of Emoluments
● Salary and allowances cannot be

Term, Impeachment, and
Vacancy of the President
Term of the President's Office
1. The President holds office for a term of 5
years from the date he/she enters the
office.
2. The President can resign at any time by
submitting a resignation letter to the
Vice-President.
3. The President can also be removed before
the completion of the term by the process
of impeachment.
4. The President continues to hold office
beyond 5 years until the successor
assumes charge.
5. The President is eligible for re-election for
any number of terms.
○ Note: In the USA, a person cannot
be elected more than twice.
6. So far, only Dr. Rajendra Prasad (India’s
1st President) has held the office for two
full terms.
Impeachment of the President
✅ Ground for Impeachment:
● Can be removed for “Violation of the
Constitution”
● Note: The Constitution does not define
the phrase ‘violation of the Constitution’
Impeachment Procedure (Quasi-Judicial
Process)
1. Initiation of Charges:
○ Can be initiated by either House of
Parliament
○ Requires:
■ Signature of at least 1/4th
of the members of that
House
■ A 14 days’ notice to the
President
2. First House Voting:
○ Must pass the impeachment
resolution by a two-thirds majority
of the total membership
3. Second House Investigation:
○ The other House investigates the
charges
○ The President has the right to
appear and defend
○ If the second House also passes
the resolution by 2/3rd majority of
the total membership,
👉 President stands removed from
office from the date of resolution
Impeachment Process
● Nominated members of both Houses can
participate in impeachment
→ Even though they don’t vote in the
Presidential election
● Elected members of State Legislative
Assemblies & UTs (Delhi & Puducherry)
→ Do participate in election
→ Do NOT participate in impeachment
🔔Fact to Remember
● No President of India has ever been
impeached till date

Office
✅ Vacancy Can Occur Due To:
1. Expiration of tenure (5 years)
2. Resignation (submitted to the
Vice-President)
3. Impeachment
4. Death
○ 🕊️ Only two Presidents died in

■ Dr. Zakir Hussain
■ Fakhruddin Ali Ahmed
5. Other reasons, such as:
○ Disqualification to hold office
○ Election declared void
Election in Case of Expiry of Term
● If vacancy is due to term ending, election
must be held before the term expires.

Emergency Powers of the
President
The President has extraordinary powers under
three types of emergencies:
(a) National Emergency – Article 352
✅ Grounds:
1. War
2. External Aggression
3. Armed Rebellion
Powers During National Emergency:
1. Can alter distribution of financial
resources between Union & States.
2. Can suspend Fundamental Rights of
citizens (except):
○ Article 20 – Protection in respect of
conviction for offences
○ Article 21 – Right to life and
personal liberty
(b) President’s Rule – Articles 356 & 365
Also called:
● State Emergency
● Constitutional Emergency
✅ Grounds:
1. Failure of Constitutional Machinery in
state – Article 356
2. Failure to comply with directions from
Union – Article 365
Powers During President’s Rule:
1. Can assume functions of State
Government
○ Including powers of Governor or
any authority
2. Can declare that the State Legislature’s
powers be exercised by Parliament
3. Can authorize expenditure from
Consolidated Fund of the State
○ When Lok Sabha is not in session
○ Subject to later approval by
Parliament
(c) Financial Emergency – Article 360
✅ Grounds:
● If financial stability or credit of India (or
part of it) is threatened
Powers During Financial Emergency:
1. Can issue directions for reduction of
salaries and allowances of:
○ All or any class of persons in Union
Government
○ Judges of Supreme Court and High
Courts
Veto Power of the President
(Article 111)
When a Bill is passed by the Parliament, it
becomes an Act only after the President's
assent.
✅ The President has three alternatives:
1. ✅ Assent to the bill
→ The bill becomes law.
2. ❌ Withhold assent to the bill
→ Known as absolute veto.
3. 🔁 Return the bill (if not a Money Bill) for
reconsideration by Parliament
→ If Parliament passes the bill again
(with or without amendments) and presents
it again:
→ The President is bound to give assent.

Legislative Powers of the
President of India
(Under Article 79–123 of the Constitution,
reinforced by Article 111)
The President is an integral part of the Indian
Parliament, and performs the following legislative
functions:
(a) Summoning, Proroguing &
Dissolution
● Can summon or prorogue Parliament
● Can dissolve the Lok Sabha
● Can summon a Joint Sitting of both
Houses (Lok Sabha Speaker presides)
(b) Address to Parliament
● Addresses Parliament:
○ At the first session after each
general election
○ At the first session of each year
(c) Messages to Parliament
● Can send messages to either House of
Parliament on:
○ Bills pending in Parliament
○ Any other matter
(d) Presiding Officer Appointments (If
Vacant)
● Can appoint:
○ A Lok Sabha member to preside
when both Speaker and Deputy
Speaker's posts are vacant
○ A Rajya Sabha member when
Chairman and Deputy Chairman’s
posts are vacant
(e) Nomination of Rajya Sabha Members
● Nominates 12 members to Rajya Sabha
→ From fields of:
○ Literature
○ Science
○ Art
○ Social service
(f) Nomination to Lok Sabha
● Before 2020: Could nominate 2 members
from the Anglo-Indian community
● 104th Constitutional Amendment Act,
2019:
○ Abolished this provision with
effect from January 2020
(g) Disqualification Decisions
● Decides questions related to
disqualification of MPs,
→ In consultation with the Election
Commission
(h) Prior Recommendation Required for
Specific Bills
Cannot introduce certain bills in Parliament without
the President's prior
recommendation/permission:
1. Bills involving expenditure from
Consolidated Fund of India
2. Bills related to alteration of state
boundaries or creation of new states
3. Money Bills
4. Bills imposing/altering taxes or duties in
which states are interested
5. Bills that alter the definition of
‘agricultural income’ for income tax
6. Bills affecting distribution of revenue to
states
7. Bills imposing a surcharge on taxes/duties
for Central use
For State Bills:
● A State Bill that imposes restrictions on
trade, commerce, and intercourse:
→ Requires President's previous
sanction to be introduced in the State
Legislature
(i) Assent to Parliamentary Bills
Once a bill is passed by Parliament and presented
to the President, he/she may:
1. Give assent (bill becomes law)
2. Withhold assent (absolute veto)
3. Return the bill for reconsideration (if not a
Money Bill)
→ If Parliament passes it again, the
President must give assent
(j) Assent to State Legislature Bills
(Reserved by Governor)
President may:
1. Give assent
2. Withhold assent
3. Return the bill for reconsideration (if not a
Money Bill)
Key Difference:
● Even if the state legislature passes the
bill again,
→ President is NOT bound to give assent

Veto Powers of Indian
President
1. Absolute Veto
● Power to withhold assent to a bill → Bill
ends
● Typically used in:
○ (a) Private Member Bills
○ (b) Government Bills passed just
before cabinet resignation
Examples:
● 1954 – Dr. Rajendra Prasad withheld assent
to PEPSU Appropriation Bill
● 1991 – R. Venkataraman withheld assent to
MP Salary & Allowances Bill (passed
without prior recommendation)
2. Suspensive Veto
● Returns a non-money bill for
reconsideration
● If re-passed (with/without amendments),
President must give assent
● Not applicable to Money Bills
🔁 Money Bills: President can either
give or withhold assent,
but cannot return it for
reconsideration
3. Pocket Veto
● Takes no action on a bill
● No time limit prescribed in the Indian
Constitution
In contrast, the US President has 10
days to act → Hence, Indian
President’s “pocket is bigger”
Example:
● 1986 – Zail Singh used Pocket Veto on the
Indian Post Office (Amendment) Bill
○ Bill sought to impose press
restrictions
○ 1989 – R. Venkataraman sent it
back, but the new govt dropped
the bill
No Veto on Constitutional
Amendment Bills
● 24th Constitutional Amendment Act,
1971:
○ Made it mandatory for the
President to give assent to a
Constitutional Amendment Bill
○ Hence, no veto power applies
Presidential Veto Over State
Legislation
General Rule:
● A bill passed by a State Legislature
becomes law only when it receives the
assent of:
○ The Governor, or
○ The President (if the Governor
reserves it for the President’s
consideration)
Article 200 – Governor's
Options on State Bills
When a bill is presented to the Governor after
being passed by the State Legislature, the
Governor has four options:
1. ✅ Give assent to the bill → It becomes
law.
2. ❌ Withhold assent → Absolute veto.
3. 🔁 Return the bill (if not a money bill) for
reconsideration by the state legislature.
4. Reserve the bill for the consideration of
the President (mandatory in some cases,
discretionary in others).
Article 201 – President’s
Options (On Reserved State
Bills)
When a state bill is reserved by the Governor,
the President has three options:
1. ✅ Give assent to the bill → It becomes
law.
2. ❌ Withhold assent → Absolute veto.
3. 🔁 Direct the Governor to return the bill
(if not a money bill) for reconsideration
by the State Legislature.
If the State Legislature re-passes the
bill and sends it again,
→ The President is NOT bound to
give assent.
→ The State Legislature CANNOT
override the President’s veto, unlike
in case of a Suspensive Veto at Union
level.
No Time Limit – Pocket Veto
for State Bills
● The Constitution does NOT prescribe a
time limit for the President to act on a bill
reserved under Article 201.
● Hence, the President can exercise Pocket
Veto for State Legislation as well.

❌ No ❌ No
Key Takeaways:
● The President’s veto over state bills is

Ordinance-Making Power of
the President (Article 123)
Definition
● The President can promulgate
ordinances when Parliament is not in
session.
● Ordinances have the same force and
effect as laws passed by Parliament.
● They are temporary laws enacted to
address urgent or unforeseen matters.
Limitations on the President’s
Ordinance Power
1. Timing Constraint (Parliament
Recess)
● Ordinance can only be issued when:
○ Both Houses are not in session, or
○ Even if one House is not in
session
→ Because lawmaking needs both
Houses
● If both Houses are in session, ordinance is
void
Not a parallel law-making power
2. Satisfaction of President (Subject to
Judicial Review)
● President must be satisfied that immediate
action is necessary
● Cooper Case (1970):
○ Supreme Court ruled that
President’s satisfaction is
justiciable on grounds of mala fide
● 38th Amendment Act (1975):
○ Made satisfaction final and
non-justiciable
● 44th Amendment Act (1978):
○ Revoked this protection → Now
justiciable again
3. Subject Matter (Scope and Limits)
● Ordinances can be issued only on
subjects on which Parliament can legislate
(Union, Concurrent List)
● Subject to the same constitutional
limitations as parliamentary laws:
○ ❌ Cannot abridge Fundamental
Rights
○ ❌ Cannot amend the Constitution
4. Parliamentary Approval Requirement
● Ordinance must be laid before both
Houses when Parliament reconvenes
● Outcomes:
○ ✅ Approved by both Houses →
Becomes law
○ ❌ Not acted upon → Ceases after
6 weeks from reassembly
○ ❌ Disapproved by both Houses
→ Ceases immediately
If Houses reassemble on different dates, 6-week
period counted from later date
Maximum Validity (If Not Approved):
● Up to 6 months + 6 weeks
○ 6 months = Maximum gap between
sessions
○ 6 weeks = Post-reassembly
lifespan without approval
Repromulgation of Ordinances
● President can withdraw an ordinance
anytime
● However, successive repromulgation
without placing it before Parliament is
unconstitutional
Key Supreme Court Judgments
● D.C. Wadhwa Case (1986):
○ Bihar Governor repromulgated 256
ordinances (1967–1981) without
Assembly approval
○ SC declared it unconstitutional
○ Held that ordinances cannot
substitute legislative process
Additional Characteristics of
Ordinances
● Can be retrospective
● Can modify, repeal, or amend:
○ Acts of Parliament
○ Other ordinances
○ Even tax laws
● Cannot be used to amend the Constitution
Binding Nature & Role of
Council of Ministers
● Ordinance can only be issued on the
advice of the Council of Ministers headed
by the PM
● President’s power is not discretionary
Procedure in Lok Sabha
● When a Bill replacing an ordinance is
introduced:
○ A statement explaining the urgency
and circumstances of ordinance
must be attached
B.R. Ambedkar’s Justification
Ordinance-making is an emergency
mechanism
To allow the Executive to deal with
immediate situations when
Parliament is not in session
It is not linked to National
Emergency under Article 352
Global Comparison
● Unusual power not found in most
democratic constitutions
● No such provision exists in the USA or
UK
Pardoning Power of the
President (Article 72)
Scope: President can grant pardon to persons
convicted in cases where:
1. The punishment is for an offence against
Union Law
2. The sentence is by a Court Martial
(military court)
3. The sentence is a sentence of death
Nature of Power
● Independent of the Judiciary
● President does not act as a court of
appeal
● Objective:
○ (a) To correct possible judicial
errors
○ (b) To grant relief in harsh or
excessive punishment
Types of Pardoning Powers
Type Description
Pardon Completely absolves the person
of conviction, punishment,
and disqualifications
Commutation Substitution of a lighter
punishment for the original one
(e.g., death sentence to life
imprisonment)
Remission Reduces the duration of
punishment, but character
remains the same
(e.g., 2 years RI → 1 year RI)
Respite Lesser sentence awarded due
to special circumstances
(e.g., disability, pregnancy)
Reprieve Temporary stay on execution
of a sentence (usually death
sentence) to allow time for
mercy petition

Constitutional Position of the
President of India
Basic Principle
● India follows a Parliamentary form of
government
● The President is a nominal
(constitutional) executive
● Real executive power is exercised by the
Council of Ministers (CoM) headed by the
Prime Minister
Dr. B.R. Ambedkar’s Observation
“The President is like the King under
the British Constitution –
He is the head of the State, but not of
the government”
● Represents the nation, but does not rule it
● His role is that of a ceremonial device, a
seal of authority
● Bound by the advice of ministers
● Cannot do anything contrary to their
advice, nor without it
● Unlike the US President, he cannot
dismiss ministers as long as they enjoy Lok
Sabha’s majority
42nd and 44th Constitutional
Amendments
42nd Amendment Act, 1976 (Indira
Gandhi Govt)
● Made it mandatory for the President to act
as per the advice of the CoM
44th Amendment Act, 1978 (Morarji
Desai Govt)
● Gave the President the power to send
advice once for reconsideration
● But, after reconsideration, the President
must accept the advice
Real-Life Examples of
Reconsideration Power
October 1997 – Uttar Pradesh
● Cabinet advised President’s Rule
● President K.R. Narayanan returned it for
reconsideration
● Cabinet withdrew the proposal
→ Kalyan Singh Govt (BJP) was saved
September 1998 – Bihar
● Cabinet again advised President’s Rule
● President Narayanan returned the advice
● Cabinet re-advised → President’s Rule
imposed in Feb 1999
Discretion of the President
No Constitutional Discretion
● Must act on the advice of CoM
● Article 74 makes advice binding
Situational Discretion (By Convention)
President can act without CoM advice in the
following scenarios:
1. Appointing a Prime Minister:
○ When no party has a clear
majority
○ Or if PM dies suddenly and there’s
no obvious successor
2. Dismissing the Council of Ministers:
○ If they fail to prove majority in Lok
Sabha
3. Dissolving the Lok Sabha:
○ If CoM loses majority, and no
alternative govt is viable
Conclusion: President’s Role
● Though the President has no real
executive authority, he:
○ Acts as a constitutional guardian
○ Exercises moral influence
○ Becomes crucial in times of
political uncertainty
● The office is meant to function with dignity
and neutrality, representing the unity of
the nation

Prime Minister of India –
Constitutional Position
● Under India’s parliamentary system, the
President is the nominal (de jure)
executive, and the Prime Minister Is the
real (de facto) executive.
● The President is the Head of State.
● The Prime Minister is the Head of
Government.
Appointment of the Prime
Minister (Article 75)
Constitutional Provision:
● Article 75: “The Prime Minister shall be
appointed by the President.”
● ❌ No detailed procedure mentioned in the
Constitution.
● Bound by convention of parliamentary
system to appoint leader of the majority
party in Lok Sabha.
Situations of Appointment:
1. Clear Majority Situation:
● President must appoint the leader of the
majority party in Lok Sabha.
2. Hung Parliament (No Clear Majority):
● President uses personal discretion.
● Generally appoints:
○ Leader of largest party or
coalition.
○ Requires them to prove majority in
Lok Sabha within a month.
Example:
● 1979 – President Neelam Sanjiva Reddy
appointed Charan Singh as PM after fall of
Morarji Desai govt.
3. Sudden Death of PM (No Obvious
Successor):
● President exercises individual judgment.
Example:
● 1984 – After Indira Gandhi’s
assassination, President Zail Singh
appointed Rajiv Gandhi without waiting for
formal party election.
Other Instances:
● Jawaharlal Nehru and Lal Bahadur
Shastri's deaths → President appointed
Gulzari Lal Nanda as interim PM until
Congress elected a new leader.
Delhi High Court Judgment (1980):
● Constitution does not require that PM must
prove majority before appointment.
● PM can be appointed first and then asked to
prove majority within a reasonable period.
Examples of such appointments:
● Charan Singh (1979)
● V.P. Singh (1989)
● Chandrasekhar (1990)
● P.V. Narasimha Rao (1991)
● A.B. Vajpayee (1996, 1998)
● Deve Gowda (1996)
● I.K. Gujral (1997)
Supreme Court Judgment (1997):
● A person who is not a Member of
Parliament can be appointed Prime
Minister.
● Must get elected to either Lok Sabha or
Rajya Sabha within 6 months, or else
ceases to be PM.
Membership of Parliament
● PM can be from either Lok Sabha or
Rajya Sabha.
● Examples of Rajya Sabha PMs:
○ Indira Gandhi (1966)
○ H.D. Deve Gowda (1996)
○ Dr. Manmohan Singh (2004)
📌 Britain's convention: PM must be
from the House of Commons (Lower
House)

Caretaker Government in India
Definition & Meaning:
● No explicit provision in the Constitution.
● It is a situational arrangement made
necessary by:
○ Dissolution of Lok Sabha
○ Or loss of confidence in the
existing government.
Characteristics:
1. Operates between two elected
governments.
2. Created when:
○ Existing government resigns/losses
majority.
○ President asks them to continue till
elections/new govt formation.
3. Objective: Ensure free, fair elections and
administrative continuity.
Functional Scope:
A caretaker government should only manage
routine affairs. It should not:
1. Initiate new policies/projects
2. Announce allowances/salary hikes/loans
3. Host official functions
4. Make major appointments or take
strategic decisions
Key Guidelines:
Tharkunde Committee (1974–75):
● Recommends caretaker govt must not:
○ Initiate new policies
○ Launch new schemes
○ Grant salary hikes or loans
○ Hold official political functions
Constitutional Commentary:
1. No formal accountability to Parliament,
as it stands dissolved.
2. Must ensure no misuse of power for
electoral advantage.
Rajeev Dhavan’s View:
“Caretaker PMs are constrained:
● No Parliament to check them.
● Cannot misuse authority to win
elections.”
Key Sources & Observations:
● J.C. Johari: "Caretaker government is a
functional necessity in the Indian system."
● Subhash Kashyap: Describes it as a
temporary holding arrangement.
● JP Movement's Tharkunde Committee:
Informally shaped the caretaker norms.
Council of Ministers &
Constitutional
Provisions
1. Parliamentary System in
India
● India follows the British parliamentary
system, but it is adapted to Indian
conditions.
● The real executive authority in India is the
Council of Ministers headed by the Prime
Minister.
● The system is described only broadly in the
Constitution through:
○ Article 74 – Status of Council of
Ministers
○ Article 75 – Appointment, tenure,
salary, oath, qualifications
2. Constitutional Provisions
(Articles 74 to 88)
Article 74 – Council of Ministers to Aid &
Advise the President
1. There shall be a Council of Ministers with
the Prime Minister at the head to aid and
advise the President.
2. President must act according to this
advice. However:
○ President can ask for
reconsideration once.
○ After reconsideration, President
must accept the advice.
3. The advice is confidential – it cannot be
questioned in court.
Article 75 – Other Provisions as to
Ministers
1. Prime Minister is appointed by the
President.
Other ministers are appointed by the
President on the advice of the Prime
Minister.
2. Total number of ministers (including PM)
must not exceed 15% of Lok Sabha
strength.
▪️ Inserted by 91st Constitutional
Amendment Act, 2003
3. A disqualified MP under anti-defection is
ineligible to become a minister.
▪️ Also added by 91st Amendment Act, 2003
4. Ministers hold office during the pleasure of
the President.
5. Collective Responsibility – Council of
Ministers is collectively responsible to Lok
Sabha.
6. Oath of office and secrecy is administered
by the President.
7. A non-MP can be appointed Minister but
must get elected/ nominated to either House
within 6 months, else ceases to be
Minister.
8. Salaries and allowances of Ministers are
decided by Parliament.
Article 77 – Conduct of Government
Business
1. All executive actions shall be in the name
of the President.
2. Instruments/orders made in President’s
name must be authenticated as per rules
made by the President.
3. President can make rules for transaction
of business and allocation of work
among ministers.
Article 78 – Duties of the Prime Minister
Prime Minister must:
1. Communicate decisions of the Council of
Ministers to the President.
2. Furnish information on Union affairs when
the President demands.
3. If the President requires, submit any
matter to CoM, even if a single Minister has
already decided it.
Article 88 – Rights of Ministers in
Parliament
● Every Minister has the right to:
○ Speak and participate in
proceedings of either House or any
Parliamentary Committee.
○ But can only vote in the House
they are a member of.
3. Nature of Advice by
Ministers
● Binding on the President:
○ Made binding by 42nd Amendment
Act (1976).
○ 44th Amendment Act (1978)
allowed President to send advice for
reconsideration.
● Cannot be challenged in court –
reinforces confidentiality and executive
privilege.
Key Judgements:
V.N. Shukla vs. Indira Gandhi (1971)
● Council of Ministers remains even after
dissolution of Lok Sabha.
● President cannot exercise executive
powers without CoM advice.
● Executive actions without aid/advice =
unconstitutional under Article 74.
Shamsher Singh vs. State of Punjab (1974)
● “Satisfaction of the President =
Satisfaction of Council of Ministers”.
● It is not personal satisfaction, but
constitutional/collective satisfaction.
4. Appointment of Ministers
● PM is appointed by the President.
● All other Ministers are appointed by the
President on the advice of the PM.
● Usually, MPs (Lok Sabha or Rajya Sabha)
are appointed as Ministers.
● A non-MP can be appointed, but:
○ Must get elected/nominated to
either House within 6 months.
○ Failing which, they cease to be a
Minister.
● A Minister can:
○ Speak and participate in both
Houses.
○ But can vote only in the House to
which they belong.
Caretaker Government
● Not defined in Constitution – a situational
arrangement.
● Created after dissolution of Lok Sabha
and continues till new government is
formed.
● Also used when government loses
confidence but President asks it to
continue temporarily.
Role Restrictions:
● Should NOT:
○ Initiate new policies
○ Announce new projects
○ Make appointments or major
decisions
Thrkundé Committee (1974–75):
● Recommended constraints on caretaker
govt actions.
1979 Example:
● President N. Sanjeeva Reddy ensured:
○ Peaceful polls
○ No new spending/policy
○ Only urgent national interest work
allowed
Judicial Observation:
● Calcutta HC (1979) – Constitutionally
undefined but functionally necessary.
Oath and Salary of Ministers
● Administered by President.
Oath of Office Includes:
1. Faith to Constitution
2. Sovereignty of India
3. Duties of Office
4. No fear/favour in justice
Oath of Secrecy:
● No communication of official matters unless
duty-bound.

● Devi Lal (1990) oath as Deputy PM

only, no constitutional status.
Salary:
● As per Salaries and Allowances of

● Equal to MPs, plus:
○ Sumptuary allowance
○ Free residence
○ Travel & medical facilities

● CoM is collectively responsible to Lok

● C.D. Deshmukh – Over state

● Arif Mohammad Khan – Over Muslim

● Ministers hold office during President's
pleasure (Article 75).
● PM can advise removal of any minister.
● PM ensures enforcement of collective
responsibility.
B.R. Ambedkar in Constituent Assembly:
"Collective responsibility can be
achieved only through the Prime
Minister's authority to nominate and
dismiss ministers."
No Legal Responsibility (Unlike UK)
● In UK, orders of King are countersigned by
ministers → Legally responsible.
● In India, no legal responsibility; courts
cannot question nature of ministerial
advice (Article 74).

Composition of the Council of
Ministers (Union Government)
Constitutional Basis
● Provided under Article 74 & 75 of the
Constitution.
● PM is the head of the Council of Ministers.
● Council is a three-tier body, based on
British conventions.
Categories of Ministers
1. Cabinet Ministers
● Top-ranking ministers.
● Handle major ministries: Home, Defence,
Finance, External Affairs, etc.
● Members of the Cabinet – attend regular
meetings.
● Involved in core policy-making and
national decisions.
● Collectively responsible for government
decisions.
2. Ministers of State (MoS)
● Two types:
○ With Independent Charge
■ Heads a ministry/department
independently.
■ Has same
functions/powers as
cabinet minister in their
domain.
■ Not cabinet members,
attend cabinet meetings only
when invited.
○ Attached to Cabinet Ministers
■ Assigned specific
departments/work under a
Cabinet Minister.
■ Works under guidance and
supervision of cabinet
minister.
3. Deputy Ministers
● Junior-most ministers.
● Assist Cabinet or MoS in administrative,
political, and parliamentary work.
● No independent charge.
● Not cabinet members, do not attend
cabinet meetings.
Special Designations
Prime Minister
● Supreme authority among all ministers.
● Leads the Council and coordinates
government functioning.
Deputy Prime Minister
● Not mentioned in the Constitution.
● Appointed for political reasons (e.g.,
coalition balancing).
● Holds the rank of Cabinet Minister.
● No additional constitutional powers.
Parliamentary Secretaries
● Not part of the Council of Ministers.
● Appointed by the Prime Minister, not by
the President.
● No control over departments.
● Attached to senior ministers to assist in
parliamentary duties.
● Function as junior political aides.
Role of the Cabinet
1. Highest decision-making authority in the
politico-administrative system.
2. Chief policy-formulating body of the
Central Government.
3. Supreme executive authority of the
Central Government.
4. Chief coordinator of Central
Administration.
5. Advisory body to the President (whose
advice is binding).
6. Crisis manager – handles emergency
situations.
7. Deals with all major legislative and
financial matters.
8. Exercises control over higher
appointments (constitutional posts and
secretaries).
9. Deals with all foreign affairs and policies.

Kitchen Cabinet (Inner
Cabinet)
Definition:
An extra-constitutional body comprising the PM
and a few trusted colleagues, friends, or even
family members (insiders or outsiders), who advise
the PM informally on critical political and
administrative matters.
Features:
● Smaller and more flexible than formal
cabinet.
● Includes both cabinet members and
non-officials (e.g., personal aides).
● Became prominent during Indira Gandhi’s
era.
Merits:
1. Efficient decision-making due to smaller
size.
2. Meets frequently and informally.
3. Maintains secrecy on sensitive matters.
4. Facilitates quick response in emergencies.
Demerits:
1. Undermines authority of the formal
Cabinet.
2. Circumvents legal and democratic
accountability.
3. Allows non-elected persons to influence
government decisions.
4. Promotes centralization of power in the
hands of PM and close allies.
Global Context:
● Exists in USA and UK as well.
● Known as “President’s Kitchen Cabinet”
in USA.

State Executive – Constitution of India
(Part VI, Articles 153 to 167)
1. Pattern of Government in States
● Constitution of India envisages same
pattern of government in the states as that
for the Centre: Parliamentary system.
● Part VI of the Constitution deals with
government in the states.
● Articles 153 to 167 in Part VI deal with the
State Executive.
2. Composition of State Executive
● State Executive consists of:
1. Governor
2. Chief Minister
3. Council of Ministers
4. Advocate General of the State
● There is no office of Vice-Governor, unlike
the Vice-President at the Centre.
3. Governor – Position and Role
● The Governor is the Chief Executive Head
of the state.
● Like the President, the Governor is a
nominal executive head (titular or
constitutional head).
● The Governor acts as an agent of the
Central Government.
● Thus, the office of Governor has a dual
role:
○ Nominal Head of State
○ Agent of Centre
4. One Governor for Multiple States
● 7th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1956:
Facilitated the appointment of the same
person as Governor for two or more
states.
Appointment of Governor
1. Mode of Appointment
● The Governor is not elected (neither
directly by people nor indirectly by an
electoral college).
● He/she is appointed by the President by
warrant under his/her hand and seal.
● Effectively, the Governor is a nominee of
the Central Government.
2. Supreme Court Ruling – Hargovind Pant Case
(1979)
● Held that:
○ The office of Governor is not an
employment under the Central
Government.
○ It is an independent constitutional
office.
○ The Governor is not under control
or subordinate to the Central
Government.
Constituent Assembly Debate – Why Not
Direct Election?
● The Draft Constitution had proposed
direct election of the Governor based on
universal adult suffrage.
● However, the Constituent Assembly
rejected this and adopted the present
appointment-based system for the
following reasons:
Reasons Against Direct Election:
1. Incompatibility with Parliamentary
System
○ Direct election contradicts the
parliamentary system adopted in
the states.
2. Potential Conflicts
○ Directly elected Governor may come
into conflict with the Chief
Minister, leading to power tussles.
3. Unnecessary Expenditure
○ Governor is a nominal
(constitutional) head.
○ Hence, elaborate election
mechanism and huge expenditure
is unwarranted.
Case Law Mentioned
● Hargovind Pant vs. Raghukul Tilak
(1979): Landmark judgment affirming the
constitutional independence of the
Governor’s office from the Central
Government.
GOVERNOR OF A STATE –
INDIAN CONSTITUTION
I. REJECTION OF DIRECT ELECTION
MODEL
The Constituent Assembly rejected the American
model (directly elected Governor) and accepted the
Canadian model (Governor appointed by Centre)
due to:
1. Personalization of Elections
→ Governor’s election would revolve
around personal issues.
2. Not in National Interest
→ Involving large electorate for such a
nominal post is unnecessary.
3. Lack of Neutrality
→ An elected Governor would belong to a
political party and lack impartiality.
4. Risk of Separatism
→ Could fuel separatist tendencies,
affecting unity and stability.
5. Undermining Centre’s Control
→ Presidential nomination helps Centre
maintain control over states.
6. Leadership Crisis
→ At general elections, parties may struggle
to find quality candidates.
7. Political Interference
→ CM may push for party loyalist; likely to
result in second-rate leadership.
Hence, Canadian model (appointment by Centre)
was adopted instead of the American model.
II. QUALIFICATIONS, OATH &
CONDITIONS
A. Qualifications for Governor (Article 157)
1. Must be a citizen of India.
2. Must be at least 35 years of age.
B. Sarkaria Commission (1983–88)
Recommendations
1. Criteria for selection:
○ Eminent in some walk of life.
○ From outside the concerned state.
○ Detached figure, not involved in
state politics.
○ Should not be active in politics in the
recent past.
2. Ruling party member at Centre should not
be appointed in states ruled by other
parties.
3. Constitution should mandate consultation
with CM via amendment to Article 155.
4. PM may consult Vice-President and
Speaker of Lok Sabha (informal,
confidential).
C. Oath or Affirmation
● Administered by Chief Justice of the High
Court (or senior-most judge).
● Contents of oath:
○ Faithfully execute office.
○ Preserve, protect, and defend the
Constitution and the law.
○ Serve the people of the state.
III. CONDITIONS OF OFFICE
1. Cannot be Member of Parliament or State
Legislature (vacates seat upon assuming
office).
2. Cannot hold any other office of profit.
3. Entitled to official residence (Raj Bhavan)
rent-free.
4. Emoluments, allowances, and privileges
determined by Parliament.
5. Salary shared proportionately if Governor
of multiple states.
6. Salary cannot be diminished during
tenure.
7. Immunities:
○ Personal immunity from legal liability
for official acts.
○ Immune from criminal proceedings,
even for personal acts.
○ Civil proceedings allowed with two
months’ prior notice.
8. 2018 Salary Update:
○ Increased from ₹1.10 lakh to ₹3.50
lakh/month
(Finance Act, 2018; effective 1 Jan
2016)
IV. TERM OF OFFICE (Article 156)
1. Holds office for 5 years from date of
assumption.
2. Holds office at the pleasure of the
President (can be removed earlier).
3. Can resign by writing to the President.
4. No fixed grounds for removal; entirely at
President’s discretion.
Judgment:
● Surya Narain vs. Union of India (1981):
“Pleasure of President is not justiciable.”
5. Post-tenure Continuation:
○ Can continue beyond 5 years until
successor assumes charge.
○ Prevents administrative vacuum.
6. Transfer/Reappointment:
○ Can be transferred to another
state.
○ Can be reappointed in same or
another state.
7. In extraordinary contingencies (e.g.
death), President can make temporary
arrangements like appointing Chief Justice
of High Court to discharge Governor’s
functions.
V. POWERS AND FUNCTIONS OF
GOVERNOR
No Diplomatic, Military or Emergency
Powers like the President.
A. Executive Powers
1. All executive actions in the Governor’s
name.
2. Can specify authentication rules for
orders/instruments.
3. Can make rules for transaction of
business and allocation among ministers.
4. Appoints:
○ Chief Minister and Council of
Ministers (holds office during
pleasure).
○ Tribal Welfare Minister (in
Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, MP,
Odisha).
(Bihar excluded via 94th
Amendment, 2006)
5. Appoints:
○ Advocate General (remuneration,
tenure at Governor’s pleasure).
○ State Election Commissioner
(removal only like High Court judge).
○ Chairman & Members of State
PSC (removal only by President).
6. Can seek information from CM on
administration/legislation.
7. Can require CM to bring any matter before
the Council of Ministers.
8. Can recommend President’s Rule under
Article 356.
9. Acts as Chancellor of State Universities,
appoints Vice-Chancellors.
B. Legislative Powers
1. Can summon, prorogue, dissolve the
State Legislature.
2. Addresses first session after general
election and each year.
3. Can send messages to the Legislature.
4. Can appoint presiding officers when
Speaker/Deputy Speaker or
Chairman/Deputy Chairman posts are
vacant.
5. Nominates 1/6th members of Legislative
Council (expert fields).
6. Nominated 1 Anglo-Indian member to
Legislative Assembly
(Repealed by 104th Amendment, 2019)
7. Decides disqualification of members in
consultation with EC.
8. When a bill is passed, Governor can:
○ Assent to it.
○ Withhold assent.
○ Return it (if not a money bill).
○ Reserve it for President’s
consideration:
■ Mandatory: If it endangers
High Court’s position.
■ Optional (per Soli
Sorabjee):
■ Ultra vires
Constitution.
■ Opposes DPSPs.
■ Against national
interest.
■ Of grave national
importance.
■ Compulsory
acquisition under
Article 31A.
9. Can promulgate Ordinances when
Legislature is not in session.
○ Must be approved within 6 weeks of
reassembly.
○ Can withdraw anytime.
10. Supreme Court: D.C. Wadhwa Case (1986)
● Repeated re-promulgation of Ordinances
violates the Constitution.
● Ordinance-making power cannot replace
the legislative process.
11. Lays reports before Legislature:
● State Finance Commission
● State Public Service Commission
● Comptroller & Auditor-General (CAG) –
State Accounts
C. Financial Powers
1. Ensures Annual Financial Statement
(Budget) is presented.
2. Money Bills can only be introduced with
his/her prior recommendation.
3. No demand for grant can be made without
Governor’s recommendation.
4. Can make advances from Contingency
Fund.
5. Constitutes State Finance Commission
every 5 years to review financial health of
Panchayats and Municipalities.
JUDICIAL POWERS OF THE
GOVERNOR
The Governor of a State in India exercises certain
judicial powers, mostly derived from the
Constitution and are analogous to the
President’s powers at the central level, though
restricted to matters within the executive power of
the state.
I. Judicial Powers and Functions
1. Power to Grant Clemency (Article 161)
○ Governor can:
■ Pardon
■ Reprieve
■ Respite
■ Remit
■ Suspend
■ Commute
○ These powers apply to persons
convicted of offences against any
law relating to a matter under the
executive power of the state.
2. Consultation in High Court
Appointments
○ The President consults the
Governor while appointing Judges
of the High Court of the concerned
state.
3. Appointment of District Judges (Article
233)
○ Governor makes appointments,
postings, and promotions of
district judges in consultation
with the High Court of the state.
4. Appointment to State Judicial Service
(Article 234)
○ Governor appoints judicial officers
(other than district judges):
■ In consultation with:
■ State High Court
■ State Public Service
Commission
II. RESERVED BILL FOR
PRESIDENT – PROCEDURE &
PRESIDENT’S OPTIONS
When a bill passed by a State Legislature is
reserved by the Governor for the President’s
consideration, the following process ensues:
A. President's Options (Article 200 and
201)
1. Assent to the Bill
→ Bill becomes law (Act).
2. Withhold Assent
→ Bill fails and does not become law.
3. Return the Bill to State Legislature for
Reconsideration
○ Must be reconsidered within 6
months by the House or Houses.
○ If the bill is passed again (with or
without amendments) and sent back
to the President:
■ The President is not bound
to give assent.
■ The President may give or
withhold assent.
B. Governor’s Role After Reservation
● Once a bill is reserved by the Governor:
○ The Governor plays no further
role in its enactment.
○ If the President returns the bill and
it is repassed by the legislature:
■ It must be resent to the
President, not the
Governor.
○ If the President gives assent, it
becomes an Act of the State
Legislature.
○ Governor’s assent is no longer
required at any stage after
reservation
ASSENT PROCEDURE FOR
MONEY BILLS – PRESIDENT
VS GOVERNOR
I. PRESIDENT’S ASSENT TO A MONEY
BILL (CENTRE)
Constitutional Process:
● After a Money Bill is passed by both
Houses of Parliament (or just Lok Sabha in
case of Rajya Sabha delay), it is presented
to the President for assent.
President’s Options (Only Two):
1. ✅ Give Assent
→ The bill becomes an Act (law).
2. ❌ Withhold Assent
→ The bill fails and does not become an
Act.
President CANNOT return a Money
Bill for reconsideration by Parliament.
Reason:
● A Money Bill is introduced in the
Parliament only with the prior
recommendation of the President (Article
110 + Article 117).
Therefore, normally the President gives assent to
the bill after its passage.
II. GOVERNOR’S ASSENT TO A MONEY
BILL (STATE)
Constitutional Process:
● After a Money Bill is passed by the State
Legislature (whether unicameral or
bicameral), it is presented to the
Governor for assent.
Governor’s Options (Three):
1. ✅ Give Assent
→ The bill becomes an Act (law).
2. ❌ Withhold Assent
→ The bill fails and does not become an
Act.
3. Reserve the Bill for President's
Consideration
→ The Governor can refer the money bill
to the President for final decision.
Governor also CANNOT return a
Money Bill to the State Legislature
for reconsideration.
Reason:
● A Money Bill in the State Legislature is
introduced only with the Governor’s prior
permission.
Hence, in practice, the Governor usually gives
assent to the money bill.

High Courts in India
Position in Indian Judiciary
● India has a single, integrated judicial
system.
● In the hierarchy, High Courts stand below
the Supreme Court, but above
subordinate courts.
● Each state judiciary includes:
○ High Court (Top court in the state)
○ Subordinate Courts (District,
Sessions, Magistrate courts)
Historical Background
● 1861: Indian High Courts Act passed by
British Parliament.
● 1862: First three High Courts established at:
○ Calcutta
○ Bombay
○ Madras
● 1866: Allahabad High Court was
established as the fourth.
● Over time, every province in British India
had its own High Court.
✅After 1950, each High Court in a province
became the High Court of the respective state.
Constitutional Provisions
● Articles 214 to 231 (Part VI) deal with:
○ Organisation
○ Independence
○ Jurisdiction
○ Powers
○ Procedures of High Courts
Common High Courts (7th Amendment
Act, 1956)
● Constitution provides for one High Court
per state, but:
● 7th Constitutional Amendment empowers
Parliament to:
○ Establish a common High Court for
two or more states, or
○ For two or more states and a
union territory (UT)
Territorial Jurisdiction
● The jurisdiction of a High Court is
co-terminus with the territory of the
state.
● For common High Courts, jurisdiction is
co-terminus with all the concerned states
and UTs.
High Courts in India – Key Facts
● Total High Courts (as of now): 25
● 3 High Courts have jurisdiction over
more than one state.
● Among 8 Union Territories:
○ Delhi has a separate High Court
(established in 1966).
○ Jammu & Kashmir and Ladakh
share a common High Court.
○ Other UTs come under jurisdiction
of respective state High Courts.
✅ Parliament can:
● Extend or curtail jurisdiction of a High
Court over a Union Territory.
Composition and Appointment (Article
216)
● Every High Court includes:
○ A Chief Justice, and
○ Other Judges as appointed by the
President.
● Strength of Judges:
○ Not fixed by the Constitution.
○ Determined from time to time by
the President, based on workload
and need.
Appointment of High Court Judges –
Procedure, Cases, and NJAC Issue
Constitutional Provision (Article 217)
● Appointing Authority: President of India
● Consultations Required:
○ For Chief Justice of High Court:
■ President consults:
1. Chief Justice of
India (CJI)
2. Governor of the
State concerned
○ For Other Judges:
■ President consults:
1. CJI
2. Governor
3. Chief Justice of the
concerned High
Court
✅ In case of a common High Court for two or
more states:
● Governors of all concerned states are
consulted.
Judges Cases & Evolution of the
Collegium System
Second Judges Case (1993)
Case: Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record
Association vs. Union of India
● SC ruled that:
○ CJI’s opinion is binding on the
President.
○ No judge can be appointed without
conformity to CJI’s opinion.
● Established the Collegium System.
Third Judges Case (1998)
Case: In re Presidential Reference under Article
143
● SC clarified:
○ CJI must consult a collegium of 2
seniormost judges of SC.
○ Sole opinion of CJI is not valid
consultation.
These two cases made “collegium consultation”
mandatory.
❌ 99th Constitutional Amendment Act,
2014 & NJAC Act
● Sought to replace the collegium system
with a new body:
○ National Judicial Appointments
Commission (NJAC)
● NJAC would have included non-judicial
members, thus reducing judiciary’s
exclusive control.
Fourth Judges Case (2015)
Case: Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record
Association vs. Union of India
● SC struck down:
○ 99th Constitutional Amendment
○ NJAC Act
❗ SC’s Reasoning:
● NJAC violated the independence of the
judiciary (part of the Basic Structure of
the Constitution).
✅ Result: Collegium System was reinstated.
NJAC struck down in 2015; collegium continues.
Qualifications of a High Court Judge
(Article 217)
To be appointed as a judge of a High Court, a
person must:
1. Be a citizen of India
2. Fulfil either of the following two conditions:
○ (a) Has held a judicial office in
India for at least 10 years, or
○ (b) Has been an advocate of a High
Court (or multiple HCs in
succession) for at least 10 years
✅ Note:
● No minimum age is prescribed for
appointment.
● No provision exists for appointing a
distinguished jurist to a High Court (unlike
the Supreme Court).
Oath or Affirmation (Article 219)
Before taking office, a High Court judge must take
an oath before the Governor of the state (or a
person nominated by the Governor).
The judge swears to:
1. Bear true faith and allegiance to the
Constitution of India
2. Uphold the sovereignty and integrity of
India
3. Discharge duties faithfully and to the best
of ability, knowledge, and judgment without
fear or favour, affection or ill-will
4. Uphold the Constitution and the laws
Salaries, Allowances, and Pension
● Determined by Parliament from time to
time.
● Cannot be reduced to the judge’s
disadvantage after appointment except
during a financial emergency(Article 360).

High Court Judges – Tenure, Removal, and
Transfer
Tenure of High Court Judges (Article
217)
The Constitution does not fix a specific tenure for
High Court judges but provides:
1. Retirement Age:
○ Holds office until the age of 62 years
○ *(Raised from 60 to 62 years by the
15th Amendment Act, 1963)
○ Age disputes are decided by the
President, after consultation with
the CJI, and the decision is final
2. Resignation:
○ Can resign by writing to the
President
3. Removal:
○ Can be removed by the President
based on Parliament’s
recommendation
4. Vacancy of Office:
○ On appointment as a Supreme
Court judge or transfer to another
High Court
Removal of High Court Judges (Same as
SC Judges)
🔹 Grounds for Removal:
● Proven misbehaviour
● Incapacity
🔹 Procedure (As per Judges (Inquiry) Act,
1968):
1. Removal motion signed by:
○ 100 Lok Sabha members or
○ 50 Rajya Sabha members
2. Submitted to Speaker/Chairman, who may
admit or reject it
3. If admitted → a 3-member inquiry
committee is constituted:
○ (a) Chief Justice or a Judge of
Supreme Court
○ (b) Chief Justice of a High Court
○ (c) A distinguished jurist
4. If found guilty, motion is considered by
both Houses
5. Both Houses must pass the motion by
special majority:
○ Majority of total membership +
○ 2/3rd of members present and
voting
6. An address is sent to the President, who
issues the removal order
✅ No High Court judge has been impeached till
date.
High Court Judges – Transfer, Acting,
Additional, and Retired Judges
Transfer of High Court Judges (Article
222)
● President can transfer a judge from one
High Court to another after consulting the
Chief Justice of India (CJI).
● On transfer, the judge is entitled to a
compensatory allowance, as determined
by Parliament.
Key Judicial Interpretations:
1. 1977 Supreme Court Ruling:
○ Transfers should be exceptional
and only in public interest, not used
as punishment.
2. 1994 Supreme Court Ruling:
○ Judicial review is allowed to check
arbitrariness in transfer decisions.
○ Only the transferred judge can
challenge the transfer.
3. Third Judges Case (1998):
○ CJI must consult:
■ Collegium of 4 senior-most
SC judges
■ Chief Justices of both High
Courts (sending and
receiving)
○ Sole opinion of the CJI is not
sufficient.
Acting, Additional, and Retired Judges
1. Acting Chief Justice
The President can appoint a judge as Acting
Chief Justice of a High Court if:
1. The Chief Justice’s post is vacant
2. The Chief Justice is temporarily absent
3. The Chief Justice is unable to perform
duties
2. Additional Judges (Article 224)
● The President can appoint additional
judges to a High Court for a temporary
period (max 2 years) when:
1. There is a temporary increase in
business
2. There are pending arrears of
cases
✅ Must be duly qualified
✅ Cannot hold office after 62 years of age
3. Acting Judges
● The President can appoint a duly qualified
person as an acting judge when:
1. A judge (other than CJ) is unable to
perform duties (due to absence or
other reason), or
2. A judge is temporarily acting as
Chief Justice
Term: Until the permanent judge resumes duties
Age limit: Cannot serve after 62 years of age
4. Retired Judges (Article 224A)
● The Chief Justice of a High Court can
request a retired judge of:
○ That High Court, or
○ Any other High Court
To act as a judge for a temporary
period.
✅ Conditions:
● Requires prior consent of the President
● Also requires consent of the retired judge
Powers & Allowances:
● Entitled to allowances determined by the
President
● Enjoys all powers and privileges of a High
Court judge
● ❌ But is not deemed a permanent judge
of that High Court
Jurisdiction and Powers of High Courts in India
High Courts are vested with extensive powers,
similar to the Supreme Court, especially within the
territorial boundaries of their respective states.
They are:
● The highest court of appeal at the state
level
● Protectors of Fundamental Rights
● Interpreters of the Constitution
● Hold supervisory and consultative roles
Constitutional Basis
● The Constitution does not provide
exhaustive details of High Court
jurisdiction.
● It states that jurisdiction shall remain the
same as before the commencement of the
Constitution, with one key addition:
○ High Courts now also have
jurisdiction over revenue matters,
which they did not have pre-1950.
Sources Governing HC Powers
The powers and jurisdiction of High Courts are
governed by:
1. Constitutional provisions (Articles
214–231)
2. Letters Patent (for older High Courts like
Bombay, Madras, Calcutta)
3. Acts of Parliament
4. Acts of State Legislature
5. Indian Penal Code, 1860
6. Criminal Procedure Code, 1973
7. Civil Procedure Code, 1908
Types of Jurisdiction and Powers of
High Court
1. Original Jurisdiction
● Limited and specific.
● Includes:
○ Election petitions under
Representation of the People Act,
1951
○ Matters relating to company law,
marriage/divorce, admiralty,
contempt of court, etc.
2. Writ Jurisdiction (Article 226)
● Can issue writs for:
○ Enforcement of Fundamental
Rights
○ Any other legal right
● Broader than the SC’s writ jurisdiction under
Article 32, which is limited to Fundamental
Rights only.
3. Appellate Jurisdiction
● Hears appeals from:
○ Civil courts
○ Criminal courts
○ Tribunals (as per laws)
● May include both civil and criminal
appellate jurisdiction, depending on the
state laws.
4. Supervisory Jurisdiction (Article 227)
● General power of superintendence over
all courts and tribunals (except military
courts) within its territory.
● Can:
○ Call for records
○ Issue general instructions
○ Ensure proper administration of
justice
5. Control Over Subordinate Courts (Article 235)
● Includes:
○ Appointments, postings,
promotions, transfers, and
disciplinary control over district
judges and other judicial officers
(excluding those under
administrative tribunals).
6. Court of Record
● Judgments, proceedings, and records have
evidentiary value.
● Can punish for contempt of itself.
7. Judicial Review
● Power to examine constitutionality of
legislative enactments and executive
actions within the state.
● Can strike down laws/orders if found ultra
vires the Constitution.
Jurisdiction and Powers of High Court
1. Original Jurisdiction
Original jurisdiction means the power to hear
matters directly, not by way of appeal.
✅ High Court's Original Jurisdiction includes:
● (a) Election disputes involving MPs and
MLAs (under Representation of the People
Act)
● (b) Revenue matters or actions done in
connection with revenue collection
● (c) Enforcement of Fundamental Rights
(through writs under Article 226)
● (d) Constitutional interpretation in cases
transferred from subordinate courts
● (e) Original civil jurisdiction (in
higher-value cases) is held by:
○ Bombay, Calcutta, Madras, and
Delhi High Courts
❌ Original criminal jurisdiction of these High
Courts was abolished by Criminal Procedure
Code, 1973
2. Writ Jurisdiction (Article 226)
Empowers High Courts to issue writs for:
● Enforcement of Fundamental Rights
● Enforcement of any other legal right
(ordinary legal rights)
✅ Writs include:
● Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Certiorari,
Prohibition, Quo Warranto
✅ Can issue writs:
● To any person, authority, or government
within its jurisdiction
● To outside its territory if the cause of
action arises within its jurisdiction

3. Appellate Jurisdiction
High Courts are primarily appellate courts. They
hear appeals from subordinate courts in both
civil and criminal matters.
(a) Civil Appellate Jurisdiction
1. First Appeals
○ From District Courts and other
subordinate courts
○ On questions of law and fact
○ Must meet the monetary threshold
2. Second Appeals
○ Only on substantial questions of
law
3. Intra-Court Appeals
○ In some High Courts, a judgment by
a Single Judge can be appealed
before a Division Bench of the
same court
4. Tribunal Appeals
○ Appeals from administrative
tribunals can be heard by a
Division Bench of the High Court
○ Chandrakumar Case (1997):
Tribunals are subject to writ
jurisdiction of High Courts
○ Person must first go to HC, not SC
directly
(b) Criminal Appellate Jurisdiction
1. Appeals from Sessions Courts
○ If sentence is more than 7 years
imprisonment
○ Death penalty must be confirmed
by the High Court, even without
appeal
2. Other Appeals under CrPC
○ Appeals lie from:
■ Assistant Sessions Judge
■ Metropolitan Magistrates
■ Other Magistrates
○ Subject to conditions under the
Criminal Procedure Code, 1973
Supervisory Jurisdiction and Control over
Subordinate Courts – High Court
1. Supervisory Jurisdiction (Article 227)
A High Court has superintendence over all
courts and tribunals within its territorial
jurisdiction, except military courts or tribunals.
✅ Powers Include:
● (a) Call for returns from subordinate courts
and tribunals
● (b) Frame rules and prescribe forms for
their proceedings
● (c) Prescribe formats for books, entries,
and accounts maintained by them
● (d) Settle fee structures for sheriffs, clerks,
officers, and legal practitioners
Scope and Nature:
1. Covers both administrative and judicial
supervision
2. Applies to all courts and tribunals,
whether or not they fall under appellate
jurisdiction
3. Can be exercised suo motu (on its own
initiative)
4. Also includes revisional jurisdiction
Not Unlimited Power
● Used only in exceptional cases, such as:
○ (i) Excess of jurisdiction
○ (ii) Violation of natural justice
○ (iii) Error of law
○ (iv) Disregard of binding
precedents
○ (v) Perverse findings
○ (vi) Manifest injustice
2. Control Over Subordinate Courts
(Article 235)
High Courts also exercise administrative and
disciplinary control over the judicial services of
the state, excluding direct control over district
judges (where Governor is involved).
✅ Administrative Powers:
● (a) Consulted by the Governor in:
○ Appointment
○ Posting
○ Promotion of District Judges
○ Appointment to state judicial
services (excluding district judges)
● (b) Direct control over judicial officers
below district judges:
○ Postings
○ Promotions
○ Leave
○ Transfers
○ Disciplinary actions
✅ Judicial Functions:
● (c) Withdrawal of cases from subordinate
courts involving:
○ Substantial question of
constitutional law
○ HC can:
■ Dispose of the case itself, or
■ Decide the legal question
and send the case back with
its ruling
● (d) Binding Nature of High Court
Judgements:
○ The law declared by a High Court
is binding on all subordinate courts
under its jurisdiction
○ Analogous to the binding effect of
Supreme Court judgments across
India (Article 141)
High Court as a Court of Record and Judicial
Review Authority
A. Court of Record (Article 215)
A High Court is a Court of Record, meaning it
possesses two key powers:
✅ (a) Perpetual Record of Judgements
● Its judgements, proceedings, and acts
are:
○ Recorded for perpetual memory
○ Hold evidentiary value
○ Cannot be questioned in
subordinate courts
● They serve as legal precedents and
authoritative references
✅ (b) Contempt of Court
● High Courts have the power to punish for
contempt of itself
● Also has the power to punish for contempt
of subordinate courts
Contempt of Courts Act, 1971 – Definition:
1. Civil Contempt:
○ Wilful disobedience to a court’s
order or breach of an undertaking
2. Criminal Contempt:
○ Any act that:
■ Scandalises or lowers the
authority of a court
■ Prejudices or interferes
with a judicial proceeding
■ Obstructs the administration
of justice
However, a High Court cannot take cognizance of
contempt of a subordinate court if the act is already
punishable under the IPC.
Review Power (Implicit)
● Even though the Constitution does not
explicitly provide High Courts with the
power of review, courts have held that as
Courts of Record, they can review and
correct their own judgements.
● In contrast, the Supreme Court is
explicitly granted the power of review
under Article 137.
B. Power of Judicial Review
Judicial Review = The power to examine the
constitutional validity of:
● Legislative enactments (laws)
● Executive orders
● Of both Centre and State governments
✅ If found ultra vires (beyond the
Constitution), the High Court can:
● Declare them illegal, unconstitutional,
and void
● Such laws/orders then cannot be enforced
Legal Basis for Judicial Review
Though the term "judicial review" isn’t mentioned in
the Constitution, it's implicitly derived from:
● Article 13 – Laws inconsistent with
Fundamental Rights are void
● Article 226 – Enables HC to issue writs for
enforcement of rights
Grounds for Judicial Review by High
Courts:
1. Violation of Fundamental Rights (Part III)
2. Lack of Legislative Competence
○ The body enacting the law lacks
constitutional authority
3. Repugnancy with Constitutional
Provisions
42nd & 43rd Amendment Acts Impact
● 42nd Amendment Act, 1976:
○ Curtailed HC’s power by
prohibiting them from reviewing
Central laws
● 43rd Amendment Act, 1977:
○ Restored the original position
○ High Courts can again examine the
constitutional validity of central laws
Subordinate Courts in India
I. Introduction
● The state judiciary consists of:
○ A High Court
○ A hierarchy of subordinate courts
(also called lower courts)
● These subordinate courts function under
and below the High Court in a
district-level structure
II. Constitutional Provisions (Articles
233 to 237, Part VI)
1. Appointment of District Judges (Article 233)
● Done by the Governor in consultation
with the High Court
● Eligibility Criteria:
○ (a) Must not be in service of Centre
or State Government
○ (b) Must have been an advocate or
pleader for at least 7 years
○ (c) Must be recommended by the
High Court
20th Amendment Act, 1966 inserted Article 233A
– retrospectively validated appointments and
decisions of certain district judges.
2. Appointment of Other Judges (Article 234)
● Appointment to State Judicial Services
(excluding district judges) is done by:
○ Governor, after consulting:
■ State Public Service
Commission (SPSC)
■ High Court
Typically recruited via State Judicial Service Exams
conducted by SPSC
3. Control over Subordinate Courts (Article 235)
● Vested in the High Court:
○ Postings
○ Promotions
○ Leave
○ Disciplinary control (excluding
District Judges)
4. Definitions (Articles 236 & 237)
● District Judge includes:
○ Additional District Judge
○ Joint District Judge
○ Assistant District Judge
○ Sessions Judges
○ Chief Judges of Small Causes
Courts
○ Chief Presidency Magistrates
● Judicial Service includes:
○ Persons eligible for posts below
District Judge level
● Article 237: Governor can extend the
above provisions to magistrates

IV. Powers of District and Sessions
Judge
● Acts as:
○ District Judge (civil side)
○ Sessions Judge (criminal side)
● Possesses original and appellate

● Exercises supervisory powers over all
courts in the district
● Capital punishment requires

appeal is filed
Tribunals in India (Part XIV-A)
Background & Constitutional Provision
● Original Constitution → No mention of
Tribunals
● 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act,
1976:
○ Introduced Part XIV-A, titled
"Tribunals"
○ Contains two Articles:
■ Article 323A –
Administrative Tribunals
■ Article 323B – Tribunals for
other matters
Administrative Tribunals
(Article 323A)
Purpose:
● For service-related disputes:
○ Recruitment and conditions of
service
● Applicable to:
○ Central & State Govt employees
○ Employees of local bodies, public
corporations, and other public
authorities
Objective: To reduce burden on courts and
provide speedy, specialized justice
Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985
● Enacted under Article 323A
● Provides for:
○ Central Administrative Tribunal
(CAT)
○ State Administrative Tribunals
(SATs)
○ Joint Administrative Tribunals
(JATs) (for two or more states)
Central Administrative
Tribunal (CAT)
Establishment
● Set up in 1985 with Principal Bench in
New Delhi
● 19 Benches across India (17 at High Court
seats + Jaipur & Lucknow)
● Conducts circuit sittings at other High
Court seats
Jurisdiction
● Original jurisdiction over service matters
related to:
○ All India Services
○ Central Civil Services
○ Civil posts under Union
○ Civilian employees of Defence
Services
❌ Excluded:
● Defence Forces
● Supreme Court staff
● Parliament Secretariat staff
Composition
● Multi-member body:
○ 1 Chairman
○ 69 Members (judicial &
administrative backgrounds)
● Vice-Chairman post abolished (2006
Amendment)
Tenure & Age
● Chairman: 4 years or until 70 years
(whichever is earlier)
● Members: 4 years or until 67 years
● Minimum Age: 50 years for both
Appointment Process
● By Central Government
● On recommendation of a
Search-cum-Selection Committee
○ Headed by: Chief Justice of India or
SC Judge nominated by CJI
Procedure
● Not bound by Civil Procedure Code
● Guided by principles of natural justice
● Low application fee: ₹50
● Applicant can represent in person or
through a lawyer
Appeals and Judicial Review
● Earlier: Only Supreme Court could hear
appeals
● Now (after Chandra Kumar case, 1997):
○ Appeals must go first to the
concerned High Court
○ Supreme Court allowed judicial
review as basic structure
State Administrative Tribunals
(SATs)
Establishment
● Created by Central Government on State
request
Jurisdiction
● Similar to CAT → Over State Government
employees' service matters
Appointments
● By Central Government
● On recommendation of a
Search-cum-Selection Committee:
○ Headed by: Chief Justice of that
state's High Court
Joint Administrative Tribunal
(JAT)
● Can be established for two or more states
● Exercises powers of administrative tribunals
for those states

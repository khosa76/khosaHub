Indian Polity - Fundamental Duties (Article 51A)

PART IV-A: FUNDAMENTAL DUTIES

Executive Summary & Quick Overview:

  • Location: Enumerated in Part IV-A, consisting of a single Article 51A.
  • Original Status (1950): The original Constitution contained Fundamental Rights & DPSPs, but NO Fundamental Duties for citizens.
  • Key Committee: Added on the recommendation of the Swaran Singh Committee (1976).
  • Amendments:
    • 42nd CAA, 1976: Inserted Part IV-A with 10 duties.
    • 86th CAA, 2002: Added the 11th duty (Art 51A(k) - education for 6-14 age group).
  • Inspiration: Borrowed from the Erstwhile USSR (Soviet Union).
  • Scope & Nature: Confined strictly to Indian Citizens only (not foreigners). Non-justiciable & non-enforceable directly by courts without statutory legislation.


1. Origin, Evolution & Historical Background

The concept of fundamental duties reflects the ancient Indian philosophy that rights and obligations are inseparable components of a civilized society.

Q1 Why were Fundamental Duties omitted from the original 1950 Constitution?

The framers of the Constitution incorporated Fundamental Rights (Part III) and State Duties in the form of Directive Principles of State Policy (Part IV), but chose not to include citizen duties. They assumed that in free India, citizens, being rooted in traditional values of Dharma, would naturally perform their duties towards the nation. However, during the Internal Emergency (1975–1977), the necessity of explicit constitutional duties was strongly felt to counter anti-social and anti-national tendencies.

Timeline of Constitutional Evolution:
  • 1975–1977: Internal Emergency period highlights the need for citizen obligations.
  • 1976: Congress Party sets up the Swaran Singh Committee to study constitutional amendments and duty inclusion.
  • 1976 (42nd CAA): Part IV-A & Article 51A incorporated with 10 duties (Swaran Singh Committee recommended 8).
  • 1999: Justice Verma Committee set up to identify existing laws enforcing duties.
  • 2002 (86th CAA): Article 51A(k) added as the 11th duty alongside Article 21A (FR) and Article 45 (DPSP).

Global Comparison: Democratic vs. Socialist Constitutions

Constitutional Model Incorporation of Citizen Duties Key Nuances & Philosophy
Socialist Constitutions (e.g., Erstwhile USSR / Russia) Explicitly Included Socialist states accord equal statutory weight to rights and duties. The duty to work, preserve socialist property, and defend the state is fundamental. India drew inspiration from this model.
Major Democratic Constitutions (USA, Canada, France, Germany, Australia) NOT Included Western classical liberal democracies focus almost exclusively on protecting individual liberties against state power, relying on general penal and civil laws for duties.
Japanese Constitution Explicitly Included (Exception) Japan is the only major democratic constitution in the world that contains an explicit, comprehensive list of duties for its citizens.

Political & Democratic Consensus:

Although Part IV-A was introduced during the Emergency by the Congress Government, the subsequent Janata Party Government (led by Morarji Desai in 1977) did not repeal Fundamental Duties through the 43rd or 44th Amendment Acts. This demonstrated total cross-party consensus that duties are essential for national discipline and nation-building.



2. Swaran Singh Committee (1976): Accepted vs. Rejected Recommendations

The Swaran Singh Committee recommended incorporating 8 duties in a separate chapter. While Parliament accepted the core recommendation and added 10 duties, it deliberately rejected several stringent penal proposals.

Prelims Focus Which Swaran Singh Committee recommendations were NOT accepted by Parliament?

Parliament rejected three major recommendations to keep duties inspiring rather than coercive:

  1. Parliamentary Penalties: Empowering Parliament to impose penalties or imprisonment for non-compliance with any duty.
  2. Bar on Judicial Review: Prohibiting courts from declaring any penalty law invalid on grounds of violating Fundamental Rights (Articles 14, 19, etc.).
  3. Duty to Pay Taxes: Making the payment of taxes an explicit Fundamental Duty under Article 51A. (Note: Duty to pay taxes was also recommended by NCRWC in 2002, but is still not in Art 51A).


3. Philosophical Foundation & Notion of Duty in India

The concept of duty is deeply ingrained in Indian ethos through the ancient doctrine of Dharma, which emphasizes moral responsibility, righteousness, and service to community over selfish rights.

Famous Perspectives & Quotes on Duties:

  • Mahatma Gandhi: "We must focus on duties and rights would take care of themselves."
  • H. R. Gokhale (Then Union Law Minister): Stated that constitutional duties would have a "sobering effect on restless and anti-national spirits."
  • Indira Gandhi: Clarified that duties do not smother rights, but rather establish a "democratic balance" by making citizens conscious of their obligations while enforcing rights.
  • Modern Jurisprudential View: Fulfilling fundamental duties is the basic price citizens pay for enjoying liberty and protection in an organized civilized society.


4. The 11 Fundamental Duties (Article 51A Clauses a to k)

Article 51A states: "It shall be the duty of every citizen of India—"

Clause Text of Article 51A Core Theme & Prelims Critical Notes
51A(a) To abide by the Constitution and respect its ideals and institutions, the National Flag and the National Anthem; Civic Duty.
Prelims Trap: Mentions National Flag & National Anthem, but NOT the National Song (Vande Mataram).
51A(b) To cherish and follow the noble ideals which inspired our national struggle for freedom; Moral Duty. Inspires patriotism and remembrance of freedom fighters' sacrifices.
51A(c) To uphold and protect the sovereignty, unity and integrity of India; Preamble Link. Directly reflects the Preamble's commitment to national integrity.
51A(d) To defend the country and render national service when called upon to do so; National Defence. Implies readiness for national service during emergencies or conscription.
51A(e) To promote harmony and the spirit of common brotherhood amongst all the people of India transcending religious, linguistic and regional or sectional diversities; to renounce practices derogatory to the dignity of women; Social Harmony & Gender Respect. Covers two vital facets: secular brotherhood and ending misogynistic practices.
51A(f) To value and preserve the rich heritage of our composite culture; Cultural Heritage. Celebrates India's syncretic, multi-cultural identity.
51A(g) To protect and improve the natural environment including forests, lakes, rivers and wild life, and to have compassion for living creatures; Environmental Protection.
DPSP Link: Echoes Article 48A. Mentions compassion for living creatures.
51A(h) To develop the scientific temper, humanism and the spirit of inquiry and reform; Scientific Outlook. Unique constitutional duty urging rationality over superstition.
51A(i) To safeguard public property and to abjure violence; Anti-Violence. Acts as a check against bandhs, riots, and destruction of public infrastructure.
51A(j) To strive towards excellence in all spheres of individual and collective activity so that the nation constantly rises to higher levels of endeavour and achievement; Excellence & Growth. Encourages personal diligence and collective national progress.
51A(k) Who is a parent or guardian to provide opportunities for education to his child or, as the case may be, ward between the age of six and fourteen years. Added by 86th CAA, 2002.
Forms the Education Tri-Junction (FR 21A + DPSP 45 + FD 51A(k)).

The Constitutional Education Tri-Junction (86th CAA, 2002):

  • Part III (Fundamental Right - Art 21A): State must provide free & compulsory education to children aged 6 to 14 years.
  • Part IV (DPSP - Art 45): State shall endeavour to provide early childhood care and education for all children until they complete the age of 6 years.
  • Part IV-A (Fundamental Duty - Art 51A(k)): Parent/guardian must provide education opportunities to child/ward aged 6 to 14 years.


5. Classification & Typology of Duties

Constitutional experts classify the 11 Fundamental Duties across various legal and moral categories:

  • Moral vs. Civic Duties:
    • Moral Precepts: Cherishing freedom struggle ideals (51A(b)), developing scientific temper (51A(h)), striving for excellence (51A(j)).
    • Civic Obligations: Respecting the Constitution, Flag, and Anthem (51A(a)), safeguarding public property (51A(i)).
  • Positive vs. Negative Duties:
    • Positive Duties: Promoting harmony, protecting environment, rendering national service (prescribed positive actions).
    • Negative Duties: Abjuring violence, renouncing practices derogatory to women (prohibited behaviors).
  • Primary vs. Secondary Duties: Primary duties are independent obligations; secondary (remedial) duties arise upon breach of primary obligations.
  • Absolute vs. Relative Duties (Austin's Classification): Relative duties relate directly to specific corresponding individual rights, whereas absolute duties are owed generally to the State or public at large without a specific individual right-holder.


6. Relationship & Correlation: FRs, DPSPs, & FDs

Rights and duties are not mutually exclusive; they are two sides of the same coin: integral, inseparable, and correlative.

Interplay & Complementary Nature:

In liberal democracies, when citizens enjoy Fundamental Rights, they must observe Fundamental Duties to ensure rights do not degenerate into license or anarchy.

  • FRs lead to FDs: Enjoyment of equality (Art 14-18) requires citizens to observe harmony and renounce misogyny (Art 51A(e)).
  • FDs strengthen FRs: Observing environmental duty (Art 51A(g)) directly enhances the Right to Clean Environment under Art 21.

Two Theoretical Perspectives on the FR-FD Interplay

  • Perspective A: Duties as a Result of Rights Fulfillment (Liberal View):

    When liberal democracies guarantee citizens their due Fundamental Rights (equality, non-discrimination, minority protection, personal liberty), citizens naturally feel a sense of stake in society. Consequently, they automatically discharge duties of upholding unity, promoting harmony, and striving for scientific temper and national excellence. In this view, the State does its duty via DPSPs (socio-economic democracy) to enable political democracy (FRs), which naturally yields voluntary performance of citizen duties.

  • Perspective B: Rights as a Result of Duties Fulfillment (Civic Obligation View):

    Rights are not absolute and cannot be enjoyed at the cost of others or national stability. Performance of duties (respect for national symbols, environment, public property) creates the prerequisite social order, security, and peace necessary for individuals to exercise their Fundamental Rights safely. This rationale led the Swaran Singh Committee to recommend formalizing Part IV-A during the Emergency.



7. Existing Legal Provisions for Enforcement (Verma Committee 1999)

Although Article 51A is not self-enforceable by judicial writs, Parliament has enacted numerous laws that penalize non-compliance with specific duties. The Justice Verma Committee (1999) identified key legal enactments enforcing duties:

No. Statutory Legislation / Act Corresponding Fundamental Duty Enforced Key Penal Provisions & Sanctions
1. Prevention of Insults to National Honour Act (1971) Art 51A(a) — Respect National Flag, Anthem & Constitution Penalizes disrespect or burning of Flag/Constitution.
1st offence: Up to 3 yrs imprisonment/fine.
Repeat offence: Mandatory minimum 1 yr imprisonment.
2. Protection of Civil Rights Act (1955) Art 51A(e) — Harmony & Brotherhood Penalizes offenses related to caste discrimination and untouchability.
3. Indian Penal Code (IPC) / Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS) Art 51A(c) & (e) — Sovereignty & Brotherhood Penalizes speeches or assertions prejudicial to national integration or inciting enmity on grounds of religion, race, or language.
4. Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act - UAPA (1967) Art 51A(c) — Sovereignty & Integrity Empowers Centre to declare communal, secessionist, or anti-national associations unlawful.
5. Representation of the People Act (1951) Art 51A(e) & (c) — Communal harmony in elections Disqualifies candidates/MPs/MLAs for corrupt electoral practices, soliciting votes on grounds of religion, or promoting hatred.
6. Wildlife (Protection) Act (1972) Art 51A(g) — Environment & Wildlife protection Prohibits illegal trade, poaching, and hunting of rare/endangered species.
7. Forest (Conservation) Act (1980) Art 51A(g) — Forest & Environment preservation Prevents indiscriminate deforestation and diversion of forest land for non-forest use.


8. Judicial Pronouncements & Constitutional Interpretation

While courts cannot issue writs to enforce duties directly, the Judiciary uses Fundamental Duties as a vital interpretative tool.

Landmark Supreme Court Rulings:

  • Reasonableness Test under Articles 14 & 19 (SC 1992 Ruling):
    The Supreme Court ruled that in determining the constitutional validity of any law, if the court finds that the law seeks to give effect to a Fundamental Duty under Art 51A, it may consider such law to be "reasonable" in relation to Article 14 or Article 19, thereby saving the law from unconstitutionality.
  • Mohan Kumar Singhania Case (1991): SC held that duties serve as a guide for evaluating legislative intentions and administrative reasonableness.
  • State of Gujarat v. Mirzapur Fine Bull Breeders Association (2005): A 7-Judge Bench affirmed that Fundamental Duties and DPSPs are crucial interpretive tools for reading constitutional restrictions on trade/liberty.
  • Ramlila Maidan Incident Case (2012): SC emphasized that courts must maintain a balance between fundamental rights of citizens and their corresponding fundamental duties.


9. Reform Proposals: Verma Committee (1999) & NCRWC (2002)

Justice Verma Committee Recommendations (1999)

  • Sensitizing the general public and creating awareness on fundamental duties across educational institutions.
  • Reorienting school curricula and teacher education programs to inculcate duties.
  • SC Direction (2003): In 2003, the Supreme Court directed the Central Government to implement the Justice Verma Committee recommendations on duty awareness.

NCRWC Recommended New Duties (2002)

The National Commission to Review the Working of the Constitution (NCRWC), chaired by Justice M.N. Venkatachaliah, recommended incorporating the following additional duties into Article 51A:

  1. Duty to Vote: Duty to vote in elections and participate actively in democratic processes.
  2. Duty to Pay Taxes: Duty to pay taxes honestly and promptly.
  3. Family Values: Duty to foster the spirit of family values and responsible parenthood.
  4. Industrial Obligation: Duty of industrial organizations to provide education to children of their workers/employees.


10. Criticisms & Contemporary Relevance

Major Criticisms of Part IV-A

  • Incomplete & Non-Exhaustive: Fails to cover essential civic duties such as casting votes, paying taxes, and family planning.
  • Vague & Ambiguous Terms: Uses abstract, multi-interpretable phrases like "noble ideals", "composite culture", and "scientific temper" which are difficult for ordinary citizens to comprehend.
  • Mere Code of Moral Precepts: Due to their non-justiciable nature, critics describe them as moral preachings or "pious wishes".
  • Superfluous: Critics argue that citizens would respect the flag or protect nature even without constitutional listing, as standard penal laws cover them.
  • Improper Placement: Placed as an appendage to Part IV (DPSPs) as Part IV-A, rather than following Part III (Fundamental Rights), which critics argue reduces their psychological importance.

Contemporary Relevance & Modern Application

Modern Context How are Fundamental Duties relevant in present-day India?

In contemporary times, Fundamental Duties serve as a moral compass and civic warning:

  • Warning Against Anti-National Acts: Serves as a constant reminder against burning national symbols, rioting, or destroying public infrastructure during protests.
  • COVID-19 Pandemic Ruling (Bombay High Court 2020): The High Court observed that during national health crises, citizens protective of their rights must also remember their fundamental duty under Art 51A(e) to promote harmony and brotherhood.
  • Civic Awareness & Constitution Day: Annual campaigns on November 26 (Constitution Day) focus on displaying FDs in schools, universities, and public offices to foster responsible citizenship.
